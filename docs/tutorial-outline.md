## 1. Skills & processes

How can a measurement support a scientific claim?


### 1.1 Laboratory safety & choosing equipment

A safe investigation starts with knowing the hazards, the procedure, and where the eyewash, safety shower, fire equipment, and exits are. Wear splash goggles and the protective clothing required by your teacher; tie back hair, keep food away, and report spills, injuries, and broken glass immediately. Never taste a chemical or pipette by mouth. Follow the instructor’s disposal directions rather than assuming a sink is suitable.

Choose equipment for its purpose. A beaker holds and mixes; a graduated cylinder measures approximate volumes; a volumetric pipette transfers a specified volume accurately; a volumetric flask makes a solution to one final volume; a burette measures a variable delivered volume. Read a clear liquid’s meniscus at eye level to avoid parallax.


`Volume delivered by a burette = final reading − initial reading`


Common trap: A beaker’s volume marks do not make it a substitute for a volumetric flask. Follow the teacher’s procedure for heating and dilution.


Tutor explanation: Accuracy depends on calibration and on how the apparatus is used. A pipette marked “to deliver” is calibrated for its intended drainage procedure. Rinsing it with the solution prevents a residual water film from diluting that solution. A burette tip bubble makes the recorded volume larger than the volume actually delivered to the flask: part of the apparent delivery fills the tip. Connect each technique to its effect on the result rather than memorizing a ritual.


Check: Why read the meniscus at eye level? A tilted line of sight creates parallax and shifts the apparent reading.

### 1.2 Scientific method, observations & lab reports

An observation records what was measured or noticed; an inference proposes an explanation. “A blue solid formed” is an observation; “a new copper compound formed” is an inference that needs chemical evidence. Qualitative observations describe a property; quantitative observations include a number and a unit.

State a testable question and a hypothesis with a reason. Change the independent variable, measure the dependent variable, and control other relevant conditions. Record raw data before calculating. A useful lab report gives the purpose, relevant theory, enough procedure to understand the investigation, labelled observations, calculations, and a conclusion tied to the evidence. Discuss specific sources of uncertainty and explain their likely effect. A graph puts the independent variable on x and the dependent variable on y, with units on both axes.


`Slope = Δy/Δx; percent error = |experimental − accepted|/|accepted| × 100%`


Common trap: “Human error” does not identify a cause or predict its effect. State what happened and how it changes the measured quantity.


Tutor explanation: The claim–evidence–reasoning pattern separates a conclusion from its support. For a density experiment, the claim identifies the material, the evidence gives mass and volume or a graph slope, and the reasoning links those measurements to characteristic density. Repeated trials estimate variability but do not automatically remove a calibration bias. A best-fit line uses the overall data pattern; forcing it through the origin needs a physical justification and compatible uncertainties.


Check: Is a constant balance offset random? No. It is a systematic effect; repeating the same measurement does not average it away.

### 1.3 Significant figures, uncertainty & scientific notation

A measurement includes the digits supported by the instrument and an estimated final digit where appropriate. Nonzero digits are significant. Zeros between them are significant; leading zeros locate the decimal point; trailing zeros after a decimal point indicate precision. Thus 0.00450 has three significant figures. Scientific notation makes the intended precision explicit: 4.50 × 10⁻³.

For multiplication and division, round to the fewest significant figures among measured inputs. For addition and subtraction, round to the least precise decimal place. Keep extra digits in intermediate work. Counted quantities, defined unit conversions, and stoichiometric coefficients are exact and do not limit significant figures. Accuracy describes closeness to a reference value; precision describes the spread of repeated measurements.


`12.11 g + 0.3 g = 12.4 g; 2.50 cm × 3.2 cm = 8.0 cm²`


Common trap: Decimal places and significant figures are different rules. A calculator display does not establish measurement precision.


Tutor explanation: The rounding rules are practical ways to avoid claiming information the measurements do not contain. If a ruler supports millimetres, reporting a length to a millionth of a metre needs additional evidence. For an explicit uncertainty, quote the central value to a matching decimal place. Relative uncertainty compares Δx with |x|; a small absolute uncertainty can still be a large fraction of a tiny measurement. Exact factors such as 1000 mL per litre change the unit, not the underlying precision.


Check: How many significant figures are in 0.03040? Four: 3, the internal zero, 4, and the final zero.

### 1.4 Dimensional analysis, density & conversions

A conversion factor is a ratio of two equivalent quantities, so multiplying by it changes the unit without changing the physical amount. Write the starting measurement, multiply by factors, cancel units, and check that only the requested unit remains. Convert numerator and denominator units separately for rates and densities. Square or cube the entire length conversion factor when converting area or volume.

Density is mass per volume. Rearrange its definition before substituting: m = ρV and V = m/ρ. For an irregular solid that does not dissolve or react, displaced liquid can provide its volume. Temperature and composition matter when comparing densities. Use kelvin for gas-law calculations: a change of 1 K equals a change of 1 °C, but the absolute zero points differ.


`ρ = m/V; 1.00 g cm⁻³ = 1.00 × 10³ kg m⁻³; T/K = t/°C + 273.15`


Common trap: 1 cm³ = 10⁻⁶ m³. A cubic unit conversion uses the cube of the length factor.


Tutor explanation: Density is intensive: doubling a uniform sample doubles m and V, so their ratio is unchanged. On a mass-versus-volume graph, m = ρV has slope ρ. If a constant container mass is accidentally included, m = ρV + m(container) has the same slope but a nonzero intercept. Unit analysis catches many setup mistakes, but it cannot prove the chosen physical model is valid.


Check: A metal has mass 27.0 g and volume 10.0 cm³. Its density is 2.70 g cm⁻³, not 270 g cm⁻³.

## 2. The nature of matter

What changes when ice melts, salt dissolves, or iron rusts?


### 2.1 Properties, particles & physical or chemical change

Matter has mass and occupies space. A physical property can be examined without changing chemical identity: density, melting point, conductivity, and state. A chemical property describes the ability to form different substances, such as flammability or reaction with acid. Extensive properties depend on sample size; intensive properties do not for a homogeneous material under specified conditions.

In a physical change the substance’s chemical identity remains; in a chemical change atoms rearrange into different substances. Melting ice changes the arrangement and motion of H₂O molecules, while electrolysis produces H₂ and O₂. Colour change, bubbles, light, or a temperature change can suggest reaction, but each also has possible physical causes. Interpret several observations together with the chemical context.


`Physical change: H₂O(s) → H₂O(l) | Chemical change: 2H₂O(l) → 2H₂(g) + O₂(g)`


Common trap: Boiling produces bubbles without forming a new substance. Dissolving is not automatically a chemical reaction.


Tutor explanation: The particle model connects visible behaviour to invisible structure. Solids have particles localized around positions; liquids have close particles able to move past one another; gases have widely separated particles and fill their container. A molecular particle does not expand to fill a gas container; the distances between particles increase. Dissolving an ionic solid separates and hydrates pre-existing ions, so it needs a more careful particle description than “the salt disappears.”


Check: When water boils, does each water molecule become larger? No; molecular spacing increases as gas forms.

### 2.2 Energy, phase changes & heating curves

Temperature measures thermal condition and is related to average particle kinetic energy in a simple particle model. Heat is energy transferred because of a temperature difference. On a heating curve at constant pressure, sloping portions describe warming within one phase; flat portions describe phase change of a pure substance at its transition temperature.

During melting or boiling, added energy changes particle interactions while the temperature remains approximately constant until the transition is complete. Freezing and condensation release energy to the surroundings; melting, vaporization, and sublimation absorb energy. The reverse paths undo those energy changes. Mixtures and changing pressure can shift or broaden transition temperatures. Use the axes: a curve plotted against time assumes a roughly constant heating power if time is to represent added energy.


`Within one phase: q = mcΔT | During a transition: q = nΔH_transition`


Common trap: A flat region does not mean no energy enters. It means added energy is supporting phase change rather than increasing temperature.


Tutor explanation: Sketch a boundary around the sample. During warming, energy raises its internal energy and temperature. During a phase transition, the relative amounts of two phases change at the transition temperature. q = mcΔT assumes no phase transition and an approximately constant specific heat. Adding warming and transition terms gives a piecewise energy budget. The ideal plateau describes a pure substance at fixed pressure; a real experiment may show heat loss and a tilted plateau.


Check: Why can steam burn more severely than the same mass of water at 100 °C? Condensation can release additional energy before the resulting water cools.

### 2.3 Pure substances, mixtures & separation methods

A pure substance has a characteristic composition: an element contains one kind of atom, and a compound contains elements chemically combined in a fixed ratio. A mixture has variable composition. A homogeneous mixture has uniform composition at the scale of observation; a heterogeneous mixture has distinguishable regions. A solution is homogeneous; a suspension can settle; a colloid has dispersed particles that can scatter light without readily settling.

Separation exploits a difference in physical properties. Filter an insoluble solid from liquid; use a separatory funnel for immiscible liquids; evaporate solvent or crystallize solute; distil using volatility differences; separate chromatographically using different attractions to stationary and mobile phases. Sieving separates solids by particle size. Magnetic separation, centrifugation, and sublimation are useful when the relevant property differs. Recovering both water and salt calls for distillation rather than simply boiling away the water.


`R_f = distance travelled by solute / distance travelled by solvent front`


Common trap: A filter does not normally remove dissolved salt from water. Separation of a mixture differs from chemical decomposition of a compound.


Tutor explanation: In chromatography a component repeatedly partitions between the stationary and moving phases. Greater affinity for the moving phase generally carries it farther. Measure both distances from the same baseline and mark the solvent front promptly. R_f has no units and is comparable only under matching conditions. In fractional distillation, repeated condensation and vaporization improve separation of liquids with relatively close boiling points; one simple boiling-point rule cannot resolve every mixture.


Check: A spot moves 3.2 cm while the solvent front moves 8.0 cm. R_f = 0.40, with no unit.

### 2.4 Ionic & molecular naming: the November 22 chart

First classify the formula. For an ionic compound, name the cation and then the anion. A monatomic anion ends in -ide. If the metal can have different charges, infer its charge from the formula and give a Roman numeral: FeCl₃ is iron(III) chloride. A Roman numeral gives the metal’s oxidation state in the compound, not the number of metal atoms.

For a binary molecular compound of nonmetals, use prefixes to show atom counts: mono, di, tri, tetra, penta, hexa, hepta, octa, nona, deca. Usually omit mono- from the first element and end the second in -ide: CO is carbon monoxide, N₂O₄ is dinitrogen tetroxide. For ionic formulas, balance charges in the smallest whole-number ratio. Keep a polyatomic ion’s internal subscripts and use parentheses if more than one is needed.


`Ca²⁺ + 2NO₃⁻ → Ca(NO₃)₂ | Fe₂O₃: 2x + 3(−2) = 0, so x = +3`


Common trap: Prefixes count atoms in molecular names; ionic names use charge balance. Ca(NO₃)₂ is not CaNO₆.


Tutor explanation: The scanned November 22 flowchart separates binary ionic, multivalent ionic, polyatomic ionic, and molecular naming. Rebuild the decision from particles: an ionic formula records the neutral ratio of charged species in a lattice; a molecular formula counts atoms in a molecule. NH₄Cl shows why “metal plus nonmetal” is a useful first clue but not a complete definition: ammonium is a polyatomic cation. In Cu₃N₂, two N³⁻ require +6 total, so each Cu is +2 and the name is copper(II) nitride.


Check: Name Ni₂O₃. Oxygen contributes −6 in total, so each Ni is +3: nickel(III) oxide.

### 2.5 Polyatomic ions, acids & hydrates

Recognize common ions as units: NH₄⁺ ammonium, OH⁻ hydroxide, NO₃⁻ nitrate, NO₂⁻ nitrite, SO₄²⁻ sulfate, SO₃²⁻ sulfite, CO₃²⁻ carbonate, PO₄³⁻ phosphate, and CH₃COO⁻ acetate. Their names and internal formulas remain when they form salts. For example, (NH₄)₂SO₄ is ammonium sulfate.

Binary aqueous acids commonly use hydro- + root + -ic acid: HCl(aq) is hydrochloric acid, while HCl(g) is hydrogen chloride. For oxyacids, -ate becomes -ic and -ite becomes -ous: HNO₃ is nitric acid; HNO₂ is nitrous acid. Retain per- and hypo- where relevant. A hydrate contains a specified ratio of water in its crystal: CuSO₄·5H₂O is copper(II) sulfate pentahydrate. Include those waters when calculating its molar mass.


`Fe³⁺ + SO₄²⁻ → Fe₂(SO₄)₃ | M(CuSO₄·5H₂O) = M(CuSO₄) + 5M(H₂O)`


Common trap: Acid naming depends on the formula and aqueous context. “-ate” is an ion name, not a prefix counting oxygen atoms universally.


Tutor explanation: Oxyanion names compare related ions with the same central element; nitrate has one more oxygen than nitrite, but sulfate and nitrate do not have the same oxygen count. Charge balance applies to acids too: two H⁺ balance SO₄²⁻ to form H₂SO₄. The hydrate dot indicates composition of a crystalline substance, not multiplication of every preceding subscript. Heating some hydrates removes water; gravimetry can determine the mole ratio by comparing lost water with remaining anhydrous salt.


Check: Write aluminum sulfate. Two Al³⁺ balance three SO₄²⁻, giving Al₂(SO₄)₃.

## 3. The mole

How can a balance count atoms?


### 3.1 Isotopes & relative atomic mass

Isotopes are atoms of one element with the same proton number but different neutron numbers. Their masses differ. The periodic table’s atomic mass is a weighted average for a stated isotopic composition; it is not usually the mass number of one atom. A mass number counts protons plus neutrons and is an integer.

Convert each isotope’s percentage abundance into a fraction, multiply each fraction by its isotopic mass, and add. The fractions must total one. The average should lie between the isotope masses and closer to the more abundant one. Relative atomic mass is a dimensionless ratio to one twelfth of a carbon-12 atom’s mass; a single atom’s mass may instead be expressed in u. Use the table’s precision consistently in molar-mass calculations.


`Average atomic mass = Σ(fractional abundance × isotopic mass)`


Common trap: Use actual isotopic masses when provided. An isotope’s mass number is only an approximate mass in u.


Tutor explanation: A weighted mean counts every atom’s contribution. Imagine 100 atoms: 75 of mass 35.0 u and 25 of mass 37.0 u. Their total mass is 3550 u; dividing by 100 atoms gives 35.5 u per atom. Algebraically, f m₁ + (1 − f)m₂ = m_average can be solved for an unknown abundance. This equation works because the abundances account for all isotopes included in the model.


Check: Can an element’s average atomic mass be a noninteger? Yes; the weighted average need not equal the mass of any one atom.


Textbook: chem11.pdf#page=114


### 3.2 Avogadro’s constant, molar mass & particles

One mole contains exactly 6.02214076 × 10²³ specified entities. Name the entity: atoms of He, molecules of O₂, ions of Na⁺, or formula units of NaCl. Avogadro’s constant converts between entity count N and amount n. Molar mass M is mass per mole, found by summing atomic contributions in the formula.

Use m/M to convert mass to moles, then nNₐ to count entities. A formula subscript adds another counting step: one mole of CO₂ molecules contains one mole of carbon atoms and two moles of oxygen atoms. Ionic solids contain formula units, not discrete NaCl molecules. For hydrates, include the waters in the formula before summing mass. Using 6.022 × 10²³ in class is a rounded working value, not a redefinition of the constant.


`n = m/M; N = nNₐ; Nₐ = 6.02214076 × 10²³ mol⁻¹`


Common trap: The molar mass of O₂ is about 32.00 g mol⁻¹. Do not use the atomic molar mass 16.00 g mol⁻¹ for oxygen molecules.


Tutor explanation: A mole is a counting unit, like a dozen, with a size chosen for atomic-scale entities. Derive n = m/M from the definition M = m/n, then check the units: g divided by g mol⁻¹ gives mol. Next, mol times entities mol⁻¹ gives entities. Keeping each conversion on a separate line makes it harder to confuse the number of molecules with the total number of atoms. Numerical molar masses use the periodic table; the exact mole count does not make those measured masses exact.


Check: One mole of H₂O contains how many moles of atoms in total? Three: two H plus one O per molecule.

### 3.3 Gas volume: STP, molar volume & conditions

For an ideal gas, pressure, volume, amount, and absolute temperature satisfy PV = nRT. At fixed temperature and pressure, volume is proportional to moles, so different ideal gases have the same molar volume. The course worksheets use the common school STP convention of 0 °C and 1 atm, giving about 22.4 L mol⁻¹.

State the convention. At 0 °C and 100 kPa the ideal molar volume is about 22.7 L mol⁻¹ instead. At other conditions, calculate RT/P or use the full ideal-gas law. Match R to pressure and volume units: 8.314 kPa L mol⁻¹ K⁻¹ works with kPa and L. Convert Celsius to kelvin. The ideal approximation becomes less reliable at high pressures and near condensation.


`PV = nRT; Vₘ = V/n = RT/P; at 273.15 K and 1 atm, Vₘ ≈ 22.4 L mol⁻¹`


Common trap: 22.4 L mol⁻¹ is a gas-volume conversion at specified conditions. It is not a liquid or solid molar volume.


Tutor explanation: Dividing PV = nRT by nP yields V/n = RT/P. This shows explicitly why one universal gas molar volume needs fixed T and P. The combined gas law P₁V₁/T₁ = P₂V₂/T₂ follows only when the same gas amount is retained. For a reaction, balance the equation first: gas volume ratios equal coefficient ratios when all compared gas volumes are at the same temperature and pressure.


Check: At the same T and P, does 2 mol of He occupy twice the volume of 1 mol of O₂ in the ideal model? Yes.

### 3.4 Percent composition & empirical formulas

Mass percent tells what fraction of the sample’s mass belongs to an element. From a formula, multiply each element’s atomic molar mass by its subscript, divide by the compound’s total molar mass, and multiply by 100%. Percentages should sum to about 100%, allowing rounding.

To determine an empirical formula, convert each element’s mass to moles, divide every mole amount by the smallest, and convert the ratios to small whole numbers. For percent data, choose a convenient 100 g sample. A ratio near 1.5 needs multiplication of every ratio by 2; near 1.333 needs ×3. The empirical formula is the simplest atom ratio; the molecular formula may be a whole-number multiple. Use measured precision to decide whether a ratio is plausibly close to an integer.


`% element = (mass of element in 1 mol compound / molar mass compound) × 100%`


Common trap: Atom ratios come from moles, not directly from mass percentages. Do not round 1.50 to 2.


Tutor explanation: Mass is proportional to both atom count and atomic mass. Dividing by each atomic molar mass removes that mass weighting, leaving relative numbers of atoms. Dividing by the smallest mole amount normalizes the ratio without changing it. For 40.0% C, 6.71% H, and 53.3% O, a 100 g model gives approximately 3.33:6.66:3.33 mol, reducing to 1:2:1 and CH₂O. An empirical formula is composition information; it does not determine bonding or molecular shape.


Check: Why can CH₂O and C₆H₁₂O₆ have the same percent composition? The latter is six times the empirical unit, so all mass fractions are unchanged.

### 3.5 Molecular formulas, combustion & hydrates

A molecular molar mass fixes the multiple of the empirical formula: divide the molecular molar mass by the empirical-formula molar mass and multiply every subscript by that integer. If the factor is nowhere near an integer within uncertainty, check the composition data and assumptions.

In complete combustion of a compound containing C and H, each mole of CO₂ records one mole of carbon and each mole of H₂O records two moles of hydrogen. If the original compound contains only C, H, and O, find its oxygen mass by subtracting the recovered C and H masses from the original sample mass. For a hydrate, use the loss of water on heating and the moles of remaining anhydrous salt to find the water-to-salt ratio.


`k = M_molecular/M_empirical; n(C) = n(CO₂); n(H) = 2n(H₂O)`


Common trap: Combustion products contain oxygen from the O₂ supply too. Do not assign all product oxygen to the original sample.


Tutor explanation: Both methods are conservation arguments. Combustion tracks C and H into products; hydrate heating tracks mass into water vapour and residual salt. For a hydrate, n(H₂O) = mass lost/18.02 g mol⁻¹ and n(salt) = dry mass/M(salt). Their ratio is the hydrate number. The interpretation assumes water is the only lost material, the salt does not decompose, and heating reaches a suitable constant mass without reabsorbing moisture.


Check: An empirical formula is CH₂O, about 30.03 g mol⁻¹, and molecular mass is about 180.18 g mol⁻¹. The multiplier is six: C₆H₁₂O₆.

### 3.6 Molar concentration & preparing solutions

Molar concentration c is solute amount divided by final solution volume. With c in mol L⁻¹, use V in litres. To prepare a solution from a solid, calculate n = cV and m = nM; dissolve the measured solute in less than the final volume, transfer quantitatively to a volumetric flask, and dilute to the calibration mark using the assigned procedure. Mix thoroughly.

A 0.100 M CaCl₂ solution has 0.100 mol of CaCl₂ formula units introduced per litre. If fully dissolved and dissociated, it gives 0.100 M Ca²⁺ and 0.200 M Cl⁻. The analytical solute concentration and the concentrations of individual species are related but not identical. Concentration is an intensive ratio; amount in moles depends on how much solution is taken.


`c = n/V; n = cV; m = cVM (V in L for c in mol L⁻¹)`


Common trap: Final solution volume includes solute and solvent. “Dissolve in 250 mL water” does not guarantee a 250 mL solution.


Tutor explanation: The formula comes directly from the definition of a ratio: cV restores the total amount. In solution stoichiometry, cV supplies the reactant’s moles just as m/M does for a solid. Both routes meet at the same mole-ratio step. When two solutions mix, both solutes and volumes must be accounted for; one concentration cannot simply be copied into the final mixture.


Check: How many moles are in 25.0 mL of 0.200 M NaCl? 0.200 mol L⁻¹ × 0.0250 L = 0.00500 mol.

## 4. Chemical changes

Which reactant determines how much product can form?


### 4.1 Writing & balancing chemical equations

A chemical equation names reactants and products and states their reacting ratio. Write correct formulas first, include physical states when known, and adjust coefficients until every element has equal atom counts on both sides. Use the smallest whole-number coefficients unless a thermochemical convention calls for another basis. Parentheses belong to a formula; coefficients multiply the entire formula.

List the atom counts rather than relying on appearance. Balance elements occurring in fewer places first, and often leave H and O until later for combustion. A polyatomic group that survives intact can be counted as a unit. Molecular and ionic equations must conserve total electric charge as well as atoms. Remember the common diatomic elemental forms H₂, N₂, O₂, F₂, Cl₂, Br₂, and I₂.


`2Al(s) + 3Cl₂(g) → 2AlCl₃(s)`


Common trap: Never change a formula subscript to balance an equation. That changes the substance.


Tutor explanation: A coefficient says how many formula units participate together; it does not change their internal composition. Two H₂O molecules have four H atoms and two O atoms; one H₂O₂ molecule has two H and two O and is a different substance. Conservation supplies linear constraints on coefficients. Balancing checks consistency of a proposed reaction, but a balanced equation alone does not prove the reaction occurs under the stated conditions.


Check: Balance propane combustion. C₃H₈ + 5O₂ → 3CO₂ + 4H₂O.


Textbook: chem11.pdf#page=168


### 4.2 Predicting synthesis, decomposition & combustion

Synthesis combines reactants into fewer products, such as Mg + O₂ forming MgO. Decomposition breaks one reactant into multiple products, such as a suitable metal carbonate forming a metal oxide and CO₂ on heating. Learn the specific decomposition patterns assigned in class; formulas and conditions decide which pattern applies.

Complete combustion of a hydrocarbon in sufficient oxygen produces CO₂ and H₂O. Incomplete combustion may produce CO or carbon as well. For synthesis from elements, use ionic charges or known molecular composition to write the product before balancing. Metal oxides and nonmetal oxides can show different acid–base behaviour, but broad classifications have exceptions. Reactions involving peroxides, chlorates, hydroxides, or carbonates require the correct specified product pattern.


`CaCO₃(s) → CaO(s) + CO₂(g) | CH₄(g) + 2O₂(g) → CO₂(g) + 2H₂O`


Common trap: A reaction category is a pattern, not proof of products. Apply known chemistry and the stated conditions.


Tutor explanation: The product-prediction handout asks you to combine classification with formula writing. For a metal–oxygen synthesis, first decide the metal’s relevant charge and the oxide ion charge; then neutralize the formula and balance. A carbonate decomposition is conveniently understood as loss of CO₂ from an appropriate carbonate. Hydrocarbon combustion uses conservation of C, then H, then O. Sulfur-containing or nitrogen-containing fuels need additional product information beyond the simple C/H rule.


Check: Is 2KClO₃ → 2KCl + 3O₂ synthesis or decomposition? Decomposition: one reactant substance gives two product substances.

### 4.3 Single replacement, double replacement & net ionic equations

In single replacement, one element displaces another from a compound if the activity pattern and conditions support it. Use the class activity series for metals and the halogen trend for halogen displacement. A less reactive metal does not automatically displace a more reactive one. Predict formulas before balancing.

In double replacement, exchange the cation–anion partners, then ask whether there is a driving change: precipitate, gas, or weakly ionized product such as water. Use the assigned solubility table. For a net ionic equation, split soluble strong electrolytes into ions, keep solids, gases, liquids, and weak electrolytes intact, cancel identical spectator ions, then check atoms and charge. Acid plus carbonate commonly produces CO₂ and H₂O because carbonic acid decomposes.


`Ag⁺(aq) + Cl⁻(aq) → AgCl(s) | CO₃²⁻(aq) + 2H⁺(aq) → CO₂(g) + H₂O(l)`


Common trap: Swapping ions does not guarantee a net reaction. If all species remain unchanged aqueous ions, write no net reaction.


Tutor explanation: A molecular equation can hide the actual changing species. AgNO₃(aq) + NaCl(aq) forms AgCl(s) while Na⁺ and NO₃⁻ remain dissolved. Removing spectators does not remove the need for charge balance: +1 and −1 combine to a neutral solid. Solubility rules are empirical guides with exceptions; they should be consulted rather than replaced by “all salts dissolve.” In aqueous single replacement, water can compete as a reactant, so classroom prediction patterns need their stated limits.


Check: Why are Na⁺ and NO₃⁻ spectators in the silver chloride example? Their dissolved chemical forms are unchanged on both sides.

### 4.4 Redox: oxidation numbers & electron transfer

Oxidation is loss of electrons and reduction is gain. They occur together: one species supplies the electrons another receives. The reducing agent is oxidized; the oxidizing agent is reduced. Oxidation numbers are bookkeeping values that help track electron transfer, including reactions involving covalent compounds.

An element in its free form has oxidation number zero. A monatomic ion’s value equals its charge. Values add to the total charge of the species. Oxygen is usually −2, except in peroxides and other special compounds; hydrogen is usually +1, except in metal hydrides. An increase indicates oxidation; a decrease indicates reduction. Find the changing elements and show the electron counts rather than assuming every reaction is redox.


`Zn → Zn²⁺ + 2e⁻ | Cu²⁺ + 2e⁻ → Cu | Zn + Cu²⁺ → Zn²⁺ + Cu`


Common trap: Oxidation numbers in a molecule are formal assignments; they are not necessarily actual ionic charges on atoms.


Tutor explanation: Assign bonding electrons to the more electronegative atom for the formal oxidation-state model. This lets the same conservation logic cover ionic and covalent reactions. In CO₂, O is −2 and C must be +4; in CH₄, H is +1 and C is −4. Complete methane combustion raises carbon from −4 to +4 and lowers oxygen from 0 to −2. Acid–base neutralization can happen with no oxidation-state changes, so it need not be redox.


Check: In Zn + Cu²⁺ → Zn²⁺ + Cu, which is the oxidizing agent? Cu²⁺, because it accepts electrons and is reduced.

### 4.5 Reaction energy, thermochemical equations & calorimetry

An exothermic reaction transfers heat to the surroundings; an endothermic reaction absorbs it. At constant pressure under the usual school conditions, these correspond to negative and positive reaction enthalpy changes. Choose the reacting system before interpreting a temperature rise: a warmer surrounding solution usually means the reaction released energy.

Thermochemical equations attach an energy to the equation as written. Reversing the equation reverses the sign; multiplying all coefficients multiplies the energy. For a stated molar enthalpy, name the substance or reaction basis. In a simple insulated calorimeter, estimate heat gained by the solution as mcΔT, then take the opposite sign for the reacting system. Include the calorimeter’s heat capacity when supplied and keep J versus kJ consistent.


`q_solution = mcΔT; q_reaction ≈ −q_solution; q = nΔH_molar`


Common trap: Breaking bonds absorbs energy; forming bonds releases it. An exothermic reaction releases more in bond formation than it requires overall.


Tutor explanation: Heat is transfer, while enthalpy is a state property. The shortcut q_reaction = −mcΔT assumes the solution accounts for the energy exchange and losses are negligible. If the cup absorbs energy too, add C_calΔT before changing sign. An energy profile’s endpoint difference describes reaction energy; its peak describes a barrier. A catalyst changes the path and barrier while leaving the same initial and final state enthalpies unchanged.


Check: A solution warms by 5.0 °C during reaction. Is the reaction endothermic? Usually no in an insulated setup: the reaction supplied heat to the solution.

### 4.6 Stoichiometry: mass, gas & solution pathways

Start every stoichiometry problem with a balanced equation. Convert the given measurement to moles using mass/molar mass, concentration × solution volume, gas volume/molar gas volume at specified conditions, or PV/RT. Multiply by the required coefficient ratio to obtain moles of the target substance, then convert to the requested mass, volume, or concentration.

For Cu + 2AgNO₃ → Cu(NO₃)₂ + 2Ag, two moles of silver nitrate react with one mole of copper. The coefficients relate moles or particle counts, not gram amounts. Read whether the problem gives an available amount or an amount that actually reacts. In solution reactions use the solute’s moles, not the mass of the whole solution. If multiple reactants have specified amounts, a limiting-reactant check is necessary.


`m_A/M_A → n_A → n_A(b/a) = n_B → m_B = n_BM_B`


Common trap: A balanced coefficient ratio is not a mass ratio. Convert through moles.


Tutor explanation: For aA + bB → cC, one reaction advance consumes a mol A for each c mol C formed. Thus n(C) = n(A)c/a if A is fully consumed and enough B is present. This is conservation expressed in batch units. The same middle step connects all the November 8 question types: solids supply m/M, solutions supply cV, and gases supply V/Vₘ. Writing units on each arrow exposes a reversed ratio immediately.


Check: For 2KClO₃ → 2KCl + 3O₂, 4.00 mol KClO₃ can form 6.00 mol O₂ with complete conversion.

### 4.7 Limiting reactants, excess, purity & percentage yield

Convert every available reactant to moles, divide each by its coefficient, and compare. The smallest n/coefficient determines the maximum reaction extent and is the limiting reactant. Calculate product from that extent. Find excess remaining by subtracting the amount consumed from the initial amount, using the balanced ratio.

Theoretical yield assumes complete conversion of the limiting reactant into the stated product. Percentage yield compares measured actual product with that theoretical amount. Purity is a different ratio: if only 60.0% of a sample is reactive material, use 0.600 times its mass before doing stoichiometry. In a reverse yield problem, required theoretical production equals desired actual production divided by the fractional yield; then work backward to the needed reactant.


`ξ_max = min(nᵢ/νᵢ); % yield = actual/theoretical × 100%; m_pure = fraction_pure × m_sample`


Common trap: A yield above 100% usually signals wet or impure product, a measurement issue, or a wrong model. It is not evidence that atoms were created.


Tutor explanation: The limiting test compares complete “reaction batches,” not raw masses or raw mole amounts. In N₂ + 3H₂ → 2NH₃, 2 mol N₂ permits 2 mol reaction progress but 3 mol H₂ permits only 1 mol. H₂ therefore limits formation to 2 mol NH₃, leaving 1 mol N₂. Purity modifies the initial usable inventory; percentage yield modifies how much product is recovered. Apply them at different stages.


Check: For a reaction needing 1 mol N₂ per 3 mol H₂, do equal initial moles mean neither is limiting? No. H₂ is limiting.

## 5. Investigating atoms

Why does an excited gas emit only certain colours?


### 5.1 Atomic models: Dalton to Rutherford

Dalton’s atomic theory connected chemical composition with atoms combining in simple ratios. Thomson’s cathode-ray work showed that atoms contain negatively charged electrons, so an atom could not remain an indivisible solid sphere. Rutherford’s scattering experiments showed that most alpha particles passed through thin foil while a small fraction deflected strongly: positive charge and most mass must be concentrated in a very small nucleus.

Later work established protons and neutrons in the nucleus. The modern atom contains a small, dense, positively charged nucleus and an extended electron distribution. Models are judged by the observations they explain and the predictions they make. A model can remain useful for one task after a more complete model replaces it for others.


`Atom: nucleus (protons + neutrons) surrounded by an electron probability distribution`


Common trap: The gold-foil experiment supports a concentrated nucleus. It does not establish literal planetary electron paths.


Tutor explanation: A scattered positive alpha particle experiences electric repulsion near concentrated positive charge. If positive charge were spread throughout the atom, large sudden deflections would be much less likely. The fact that most particles passed through constrains how small the scattering region is relative to the atom. Nuclear stability involves the strong nuclear interaction competing with proton–proton electrical repulsion; neutrons do not act as a simple electrostatic glue.


Check: Which finding challenged Thomson’s diffuse-positive-charge model? The rare large-angle deflections of alpha particles.

### 5.2 Subatomic particles, nuclide symbols & ions

Atomic number Z counts protons and identifies the element. Mass number A counts protons plus neutrons in a specific isotope, so neutrons = A − Z. A neutral atom has Z electrons. A positive ion has lost electrons; a negative ion has gained electrons. Ion formation normally changes electron number without changing the nucleus.

Write a nuclide using the mass number at upper left, atomic number at lower left, and ion charge at upper right. For ²³Na⁺, Z = 11, A = 23, so there are 11 protons, 12 neutrons, and 10 electrons. Use an explicitly specified isotope to count neutrons. A periodic-table average atomic mass does not uniquely specify the isotope of a particular atom.


`p = Z; neutrons = A − Z; electrons = Z − q (q is signed ion charge)`


Common trap: Do not round a periodic-table average and present it as a uniquely known mass number unless the problem explicitly asks for that approximation.


Tutor explanation: Charge in elementary-charge units is q = number of protons − number of electrons. Rearranging gives e = Z − q. This handles both signs: O²⁻ has 8 − (−2) = 10 electrons, while Al³⁺ has 13 − 3 = 10. Species with the same electron count are isoelectronic, but different nuclear charges give different sizes and energies. An element’s identity follows its proton count, so adding a neutron makes an isotope; adding an electron makes an ion.


Check: How many particles are in ³⁵Cl⁻? 17 protons, 18 neutrons, and 18 electrons.

### 5.3 Waves, photons, spectra & the Bohr model

Wavelength λ measures the distance between repeating wave positions; frequency ν counts cycles per second; amplitude measures the size of the oscillation. For light in vacuum, c = λν. Shorter wavelength therefore means higher frequency. Photon energy is proportional to frequency, so shorter-wavelength photons carry greater energy.

In the Bohr model of hydrogen, electrons have discrete allowed energies. Absorption raises the atom’s energy by an allowed amount; emission releases a photon whose energy matches a downward energy difference. These discrete differences explain a line spectrum rather than every possible wavelength. White light can show a continuous spectrum, while different elements show characteristic emission patterns. The Bohr model works especially well for one-electron species, and needs replacement for detailed many-electron behaviour.


`c = λν; E_photon = hν = hc/λ; |ΔE_atom| = E_photon`


Common trap: Amplitude is not frequency. Greater light intensity need not mean greater energy per photon when the frequency stays fixed.


Tutor explanation: A spectral line is an energy difference, not the energy of a single electron level measured from an arbitrary zero. For emission, ΔE_atom is negative while emitted photon energy is positive. If λ = 500 nm, convert to 5.00 × 10⁻⁷ m: E ≈ 3.97 × 10⁻¹⁹ J per photon. Multiplying by Nₐ gives energy per mole of photons. In a wave-on-a-string activity, changing frequency at fixed wave speed changes wavelength; changing amplitude is a separate control.


Check: Which photon carries more energy, violet or red? Violet, because its wavelength is shorter and frequency higher.

### 5.4 Orbitals, quantum numbers & electron capacity

An orbital describes a quantum state and its electron probability distribution, not a track followed by a tiny planet. The principal quantum number n relates to shell and size; the angular quantum number ℓ identifies subshell type; mℓ distinguishes orbitals within a subshell; electron spin has two allowed projections.

For a given n, ℓ can run from 0 to n − 1. The labels s, p, d, and f correspond to ℓ = 0, 1, 2, and 3. A subshell has 2ℓ + 1 orbitals, and each orbital holds at most two electrons with opposite spins. Thus s, p, d, and f hold 2, 6, 10, and 14 electrons. s probability distributions are spherical; p orbitals have two lobes separated by a nodal plane. The sign of a wavefunction lobe is not electric charge.


`n = 1,2,…; ℓ = 0,…,n−1; mℓ = −ℓ,…,+ℓ; mₛ = ±½`


Common trap: A p subshell contains three orbitals. One p orbital holds two electrons, not six.


Tutor explanation: Counting states explains capacities: mℓ has 2ℓ + 1 distinct values and each supports two spin states. Summing capacities over all subshells in shell n gives 2n², a maximum shell capacity rather than the neutral-atom filling order. A stylized orbital drawing often shows a boundary enclosing a chosen probability, not a hard wall. Shape alone may identify s/p/d character; the principal number usually requires more information such as radial-node count or a label.


Check: Why is there no 1p subshell? At n = 1, the only allowed ℓ is 0, which is s.

### 5.5 Electron configurations, ions & exceptions

Fill lower-energy states first in the usual Aufbau guide: 1s, 2s, 2p, 3s, 3p, 4s, 3d, 4p, 5s, 4d, 5p, 6s, 4f, 5d, 6p. Pauli exclusion permits at most two opposite-spin electrons per orbital. Hund’s rule fills equivalent-energy orbitals singly with parallel spins before pairing. Core notation replaces filled inner shells with the preceding noble-gas symbol.

Check that all superscripts sum to the species’ electron count. For transition-metal cations, remove the highest principal-shell s electrons before the d electrons: Fe is [Ar]3d⁶4s² and Fe²⁺ is [Ar]3d⁶. Cr and Cu are familiar neutral exceptions: [Ar]3d⁵4s¹ and [Ar]3d¹⁰4s¹. More exceptions exist; use a reliable configuration when the question supplies one.


`P: [Ne]3s²3p³ | Cu: [Ar]3d¹⁰4s¹ | Cu²⁺: [Ar]3d⁹`


Common trap: The filling guide for neutral atoms is not a rule to remove the most recently written electron first. Equal electron counts do not always imply equal configurations for transition ions.


Tutor explanation: Orbital energies depend on occupation and nuclear charge. “4s fills before 3d” in a neutral-atom building sequence does not mean 4s stays lower for every ion. The November notes also use a course convention for valence electrons: highest-shell s/p plus electrons in incomplete d/f subshells. State that convention for transition metals; main-group valence counts are more straightforward. A diagram showing only d¹⁰ does not uniquely identify the whole species: Zn also has 4s², whereas Zn²⁺ does not.


Check: Write Cr³⁺ from neutral Cr = [Ar]3d⁵4s¹. Remove the 4s electron, then two 3d electrons: [Ar]3d³.

## 6. Relationships & bonding

Why is water polar but carbon dioxide nonpolar?


### 6.1 The periodic table, families & valence patterns

Mendeleev organized elements using repeating chemical properties and atomic-mass patterns, leaving gaps that supported predictions. The modern periodic table is arranged by atomic number. Rows are periods; columns are groups. Main-group members in a group have similar outer-electron configurations, helping explain related chemistry.

Group 1 has a common ns¹ valence pattern and commonly forms +1 ions; group 2 has ns² and commonly forms +2. Group 17 has ns²np⁵ and commonly forms −1; group 18 has a filled outer shell, with helium’s 1s² as the first-shell case. The s, p, d, and f blocks correspond to filling subshell types. Metals tend to conduct and are often malleable; nonmetal solids are often brittle; metalloids show mixed behaviour. These are trends with exceptions, not complete definitions.


`s block: 2 columns | p: 6 | d: 10 | f: 14`


Common trap: Groups share recurring valence patterns, not the same principal shell number. Hydrogen’s chemistry is not captured by treating it as an ordinary alkali metal.


Tutor explanation: The block widths follow orbital capacities, connecting the table’s shape to quantum states. Across a main-group period, the highest-shell s/p occupancy changes in a repeated sequence; down a group, the principal shell number rises while that occupancy pattern recurs. The periodic-table investigation also compares density. Since density depends on both atomic mass and solid packing volume, it has less universal trends than radius or first ionization energy; use the actual data for a density prediction.


Check: Why do Li and Na show related chemistry? Both have a common outer ns¹ pattern, though in different principal shells.

### 6.2 Atomic radius, ion size & ionization-energy exceptions

Across a main-group period, atomic radius generally decreases as increasing nuclear charge pulls the electrons in the same main shell inward. Down a group, added shells and shielding generally increase radius. A cation is typically smaller than its parent atom; an anion larger. In an isoelectronic series, greater nuclear charge generally means smaller radius.

First ionization energy is the energy required to remove an electron from a gaseous atom. It generally increases across a period and decreases down a group, but subshell energies and electron pairing create important exceptions. Mg has a higher first ionization energy than Al because Al loses a higher-energy 3p electron. N has a higher first ionization energy than O because removing a paired 2p electron from O reduces pairing repulsion. Electronegativity describes attraction for shared electrons in a bond and generally increases toward the upper right.


`X(g) → X⁺(g) + e⁻ | Isoelectronic size: O²⁻ > F⁻ > Na⁺ > Mg²⁺`


Common trap: “More protons” alone is incomplete: compare shells, shielding, subshells, and electron pairing too.


Tutor explanation: Effective nuclear attraction combines actual nuclear charge with electron screening. Across a period, added same-shell electrons do not fully screen each extra proton, so the outer distribution contracts. Down a group, a new principal shell changes the distance scale substantially. Successive ionization energies rise; a large jump after removing the valence electrons signals entry into a core shell. First ionization energy and electronegativity answer different questions: removing an electron versus attracting density in a bond.


Check: Which is smaller, Na⁺ or F⁻? Both have 10 electrons, but Na⁺ has more protons, so it is smaller.

### 6.3 Bond formation, potential energy & bond polarity

When two atoms approach, electron–nucleus attractions can lower potential energy; at very short separation, repulsions raise it strongly. The potential-energy minimum marks an equilibrium separation. The force is zero there, although atoms can vibrate around that separation. Separating bonded atoms requires energy; forming a stable bond from separated atoms releases energy overall.

Covalent bonding shares electron density; ionic solids contain oppositely charged ions in an extended structure; metallic bonding involves mobile electrons in a metal structure. Unequal sharing produces a polar covalent bond with partial charges. Use the December handout’s approximate electronegativity-difference boundaries for its questions: below 0.4 nonpolar covalent, 0.4–1.7 polar covalent, above 1.7 ionic. These are course classification conventions within a continuum.


`ΔEN = |EN_A − EN_B|; F(r) = −dU/dr; at a stable minimum, dU/dr = 0`


Common trap: Ionic and covalent character vary continuously. A numerical cutoff is a classification guide, not an abrupt change in physical behaviour; HF is a molecular polar-covalent example despite exceeding 1.7 on the handout’s scale.


Tutor explanation: At a separation greater than equilibrium on the attractive side, the force tends to pull atoms together; below equilibrium on the repulsive side, it tends to push them apart. Zero force at the minimum does not require zero speed: an oscillating pair can pass through equilibrium fastest. A well depth describes the energy needed to reach separated atoms from the minimum in that model. For the same atom pair, higher bond order generally means a shorter, stronger bond: compare C–C, C=C, and C≡C. Distinguish this well from a reaction activation barrier; they refer to different physical questions.


Check: At the minimum of a bond-energy curve, are all attractions absent? No. Attractive and repulsive contributions balance.

### 6.4 Lewis structures, formal charge & resonance

Count total valence electrons, adding electrons for negative charge and subtracting for positive charge. Choose a plausible skeleton, connect atoms with single bonds, complete terminal octets (H needs two), and place remaining electrons on the centre. Form multiple bonds when needed, then recount every electron and check total charge.

Formal charge helps compare drawings: valence count minus nonbonding electrons minus half of bonding electrons. Resonance contributors have the same atom arrangement but different allowed electron placements. The real electron distribution is not a molecule jumping between drawings. Treat electron-deficient species such as BF₃, odd-electron radicals such as NO, and expanded-octet Lewis representations such as PCl₅ as exceptions to a universal octet recipe. Second-period C, N, O, and F do not expand their octets.


`Formal charge = valence electrons − nonbonding electrons − ½(bonding electrons)`


Common trap: The shortcut bonds = (octet electrons needed − available electrons)/2 applies only to suitable ordinary octet/duet structures, not every species.


Tutor explanation: For NO₂⁻ the total is 5 + 2(6) + 1 = 18 electrons. A valid contributor has one N=O, one N–O⁻, and one N lone pair. Exchanging the two O positions gives another contributor; delocalization makes the two N–O bonds equivalent in the isolated ion. For SO₂, zero-formal-charge expanded-octet drawings are useful school bookkeeping. Modern hypervalent bonding is not explained simply by claiming that period-3 atoms use empty d orbitals to make extra bonds.


Check: How many valence electrons are in CO₃²⁻? 4 + 3(6) + 2 = 24.

### 6.5 VSEPR shapes & the molecular dipole

Use the Lewis structure to count electron domains around the centre. A single, double, or triple bond each counts as one domain; a lone pair also counts as one. Electron-domain geometry spreads these regions apart. Molecular shape describes the positions of nuclei, so lone pairs affect the shape but are not named as atoms.

Two domains give linear geometry; three give trigonal planar; four give tetrahedral electron geometry. With four domains, CH₄ is tetrahedral, NH₃ is trigonal pyramidal, and H₂O is bent. Five and six domains give trigonal-bipyramidal and octahedral electron geometries. For molecular polarity, combine bond dipoles as vectors using the actual shape and surrounding atoms. Symmetry can cancel equal dipoles: CO₂ is nonpolar, while bent H₂O has a net dipole.


`CO₂: AX₂ linear, 180° | CH₄: AX₄, 109.5° | NH₃: AX₃E, ≈107° | H₂O: AX₂E₂, ≈104.5°`


Common trap: Polar bonds do not automatically produce a polar molecule. Lone pairs and different terminal atoms can prevent cancellation.


Tutor explanation: VSEPR is a model for predicting geometry from local electron density. Lone pairs often repel bonding regions more strongly and compress bond angles in simple examples, but ideal angles are reference values rather than universal measured numbers. In CO₂, the two equal C=O dipoles point in opposite directions and sum to zero. In water, the angle prevents the two O–H dipoles from cancelling. A flat Lewis drawing gives connectivity; it is not a reliable three-dimensional geometry diagram.


Check: Why is CO₂ linear despite two double bonds? Each double bond is one domain, so there are two domains around carbon.

### 6.6 Intermolecular forces & water’s behaviour

Intramolecular bonds hold atoms together within a molecular substance; intermolecular attractions act between molecules. All atoms and molecules have London dispersion forces arising from fluctuating electron distributions. Greater polarizability and effective surface contact can increase dispersion. Polar molecules also have dipole–dipole attractions.

Hydrogen bonding commonly involves H bonded to N, O, or F interacting with a suitable lone pair on another particle. Ion–dipole attractions connect dissolved ions with polar water molecules. These interactions help explain boiling points, viscosity, surface tension, and solubility. Water’s extensive hydrogen-bond network contributes to its relatively high boiling point and heat capacity; its open ice structure makes ordinary ice less dense than liquid water. Comparisons require molecular size and shape as well as named force types.


`Na⁺ attracts water’s Oδ⁻ end; Cl⁻ attracts water’s Hδ⁺ ends`


Common trap: Boiling water mainly separates molecules; it does not break each H₂O molecule into hydrogen and oxygen.


Tutor explanation: A molecule with a hydrogen-bond donor still has dispersion and can have other electrostatic attractions. Do not replace all forces with the strongest named category. A large nonpolar molecule can have a higher boiling point than a smaller polar one because dispersion grows with polarizability. Water can hydrate ions by orienting its partial charges, but hydration must compete with the solid lattice and with water–water interactions, which is why not every ionic solid dissolves readily.


Check: Can ethanol hydrogen-bond with water? Yes: its O–H group can donate and its oxygen lone pairs can accept hydrogen bonds.

## 7. Solution chemistry

What is conserved when a solution is diluted?


### 7.1 Solubility, saturation & temperature

A solution is a homogeneous mixture of solute and solvent. Solubility states the maximum amount that dissolves in a specified amount of solvent or solution at a stated temperature under stated conditions. Read whether the units are g per 100 g water, g per litre solution, or mol per litre: these denominators are different.

An unsaturated solution can dissolve more under those conditions; a saturated solution is at the solubility limit; a supersaturated solution contains more dissolved solute than the stable equilibrium amount and can crystallize when disturbed or seeded. For a saturated solution in contact with solid, dissolving and crystallizing continue with equal rates. Many solids become more soluble as temperature rises, but exceptions exist. Gas solubility commonly decreases on warming and increases with the gas’s partial pressure in the simple dilute model.


`Solubility in mol L⁻¹ = solubility in g L⁻¹ / molar mass in g mol⁻¹`


Common trap: Saturated does not necessarily mean concentrated. A sparingly soluble compound can form a very dilute saturated solution.


Tutor explanation: A solubility curve is a capacity map at different temperatures. If a salt dissolves to 40 g per 100 g water at one temperature but 25 g at a lower one, cooling that saturated sample can crystallize 15 g per 100 g water, assuming no solvent loss and the same solid form. Solubility and dissolution rate are different: stirring can accelerate reaching the limit without changing the equilibrium limit itself.


Check: Does grinding a salt necessarily raise its equilibrium solubility? No. It typically increases the rate of dissolving by exposing more area.


Textbook: chem11.pdf#page=370


### 7.2 Like dissolves like: competing attractions

Dissolving requires separating some solute–solute and solvent–solvent contacts while forming solute–solvent contacts. Polar solvents can interact favourably with polar molecules and ions; nonpolar solvents often dissolve nonpolar molecular substances. “Like dissolves like” summarizes common patterns, while the full outcome also depends on crystal stability, mixing effects, temperature, and composition.

Water hydrates Na⁺ with oxygen ends pointing toward the ion and Cl⁻ with hydrogen ends toward it. Ethanol can hydrogen-bond with water through its hydroxyl group; a longer hydrocarbon region can reduce water compatibility in related molecules. Oil and water form separate phases because their interaction balance does not favour extensive mixing. Immiscible liquids can still have small mutual solubilities.


`Dissolution: replace some solute–solute and solvent–solvent interactions with solute–solvent interactions`


Common trap: Water is not a universal solvent. Being ionic does not guarantee high water solubility.


Tutor explanation: Use a particle sketch to test the phrase. Draw the solvent’s positive and negative regions, orient them around the solute, and ask what interactions must be overcome. A stable ionic lattice can resist dissolution even though water strongly attracts its ions. Dissolving a molecular solute such as sucrose can preserve molecules, whereas an ionic salt dissociates. This distinction explains why some concentrated solutions conduct poorly and others conduct well.


Check: Why does a hydroxyl group help an alcohol mix with water? It supports favourable hydrogen bonding with the surrounding water.

### 7.3 Dissociation, electrolytes & ion concentrations

A dissolved electrolyte supplies mobile ions that carry current. A soluble ionic salt typically dissociates into its component ions; some molecular acids ionize by reacting with water. A nonelectrolyte such as sucrose can dissolve as neutral molecules without supplying many ions. Strong versus weak electrolyte describes the extent of ion production under specified conditions, not simply whether a solution is concentrated.

Write a balanced dissociation equation and use its coefficients to convert formal solute concentration to ion concentrations when dissociation is complete. For Al₂(SO₄)₃ at 0.0200 M, [Al³⁺] = 0.0400 M and [SO₄²⁻] = 0.0600 M. For conductivity, consider concentration, ion charges, mobility, and dissociation; counting ions alone cannot compare every real solution.


`CaCl₂(aq) → Ca²⁺(aq) + 2Cl⁻(aq); [Cl⁻] = 2c(CaCl₂)`


Common trap: An insoluble ionic solid supplies few ions to water. “Ionic” alone does not establish high solution conductivity.


Tutor explanation: Charge conservation is an independent check. In 0.100 M CaCl₂, positive charge concentration is 2 × 0.100 and negative charge magnitude is 1 × 0.200, so they balance. The formal concentration tracks how much salt was introduced; brackets identify an actual dissolved species. At this level, assume complete dissociation for the specified soluble salts and ignore complex formation unless supplied by the problem.


Check: For 0.0300 M Na₃PO₄ with complete dissociation, [Na⁺] = 0.0900 M and [PO₄³⁻] = 0.0300 M.

### 7.4 Dilution, mixing & solution stoichiometry

Diluting a solution adds solvent while conserving solute amount, provided no reaction or loss occurs. Equate n_before and n_after to obtain c₁V₁ = c₂V₂. Volumes can share any consistent unit in this equation because only their ratio matters. Use final solution volume, not just solvent volume added.

When combining solutions of the same nonreacting solute, add their moles and divide by the final volume. If a reaction occurs, calculate the initial moles of each reacting species, apply the balanced ratio and limiting-reactant logic, and divide remaining or produced moles by the final volume as required. For simple school problems, volumes are often assumed additive. An aliquot transfers only the fraction of total solute corresponding to the volume taken.


`c₁V₁ = c₂V₂; c_mix = (c₁V₁ + c₂V₂)/(V₁ + V₂) for additive, nonreacting solutions`


Common trap: Diluting to 100.0 mL is different from adding 100.0 mL of water. Averaging concentrations works only for equal mixed volumes without reaction.


Tutor explanation: Derive rather than memorize: n = cV, and dilution leaves n unchanged, so c₁V₁ = c₂V₂. Mixing adds two inventories instead, giving n_total = c₁V₁ + c₂V₂. Precipitation changes the dissolved inventories; for Ag⁺ + Cl⁻ → AgCl(s), first remove the reacting amounts. The final concentration of any excess ion is its leftover moles divided by total solution volume, not its original concentration minus the other solution’s concentration.


Check: If 25.0 mL of 0.200 M stock is diluted to 100.0 mL, the final concentration is 0.0500 M.

### 7.5 Titrations & experimental technique

A titration uses a known-concentration titrant to determine an unknown amount or concentration. The equivalence point is the stoichiometric match given by the balanced equation. The endpoint is the observed signal, such as an indicator colour change; good technique aims to make it close to equivalence. A standard solution has a reliably known concentration; standardization determines that concentration experimentally.

For aA + bB → products, at equivalence n(A)/a = n(B)/b. Use cV for each amount and solve. The common c_acidV_acid = c_baseV_base shortcut needs a 1:1 reaction ratio. Read the burette consistently, remove tip bubbles, swirl, and add slowly near the endpoint. Use concordant titres according to the class criterion and follow the assigned safety and waste procedure.


`At equivalence: c_AV_A/a = c_BV_B/b; titre = final burette reading − initial reading`


Common trap: Neutralization does not always use a 1:1 ratio. H₂SO₄ requires two moles of NaOH per mole for full neutralization.


Tutor explanation: Suppose a monoprotic acid sample is titrated with NaOH. An overshot endpoint records too much NaOH and therefore overestimates acid moles if that extra delivery is treated as reaction demand. Water accidentally added to the titration flask changes concentration but not the acid moles already pipetted, so the ideal required titrant moles remain the same. A pipette diluted by residual water before taking the sample is a different error: it can reduce transferred acid moles.


Check: Why does adding a little pure water to the receiving flask not change the ideal titre? It changes volume but conserves the analyte amount.

## 8. Organic chemistry

How can the same formula describe different compounds?


### 8.1 Carbon bonding, hydrocarbons & structural formulas

Carbon commonly makes four bonds, allowing chains, branches, rings, and multiple bonds. Hydrocarbons contain only C and H. Alkanes have only single C–C bonds; alkenes have C=C; alkynes have C≡C. Saturated acyclic hydrocarbons are alkanes; unsaturation can involve rings or multiple bonds, so inspect structure as well as formula.

A structural formula shows connectivity, a condensed formula groups atoms, and a skeletal formula omits carbon labels and most carbon-bound hydrogen labels. Every unlabeled line end or vertex represents carbon; add enough implicit H to complete the usual carbon valence. The acyclic alkane formula CₙH₂ₙ₊₂ applies to a neutral saturated open chain. One ring or one C=C reduces H by two relative to that formula; a triple bond reduces it by four.


`Acyclic alkane: CₙH₂ₙ₊₂ | one acyclic C=C: CₙH₂ₙ | one acyclic C≡C: CₙH₂ₙ₋₂`


Common trap: The formula CₙH₂ₙ does not uniquely identify an alkene; a saturated cycloalkane can share it.


Tutor explanation: Count carbon valence at every position. In CH₃CH=CH₂, the middle carbon has one single bond to C, one double bond to C, and one bond to H: four bond units total. Skeletons encode the same count economically. Benzene’s aromatic bonding is delocalized and should not be treated as an ordinary isolated alkene for every reaction. Many carbon compounds are organic, but familiar carbon oxides and carbonates are conventionally treated as inorganic.


Check: How many carbons are in an unbranched zigzag with four line segments? Five: three interior vertices plus two ends.

### 8.2 Naming hydrocarbons & recognizing isomers

Choose a suitable longest parent chain containing the relevant multiple bond or priority functional group. Number to give the important features the lowest appropriate locants, then name substituents and their positions. For a simple branched alkane, use the first point of difference between locant sets. List different substituent names alphabetically; ignore di-, tri-, and tetra- for this alphabetizing step.

Learn the first ten carbon roots: meth, eth, prop, but, pent, hex, hept, oct, non, dec. Use -ane, -ene, or -yne for the hydrocarbon bond type and give multiple-bond positions where necessary. Structural isomers share a molecular formula but have different connectivity: butane and 2-methylpropane both have C₄H₁₀. Rotation about an ordinary single bond changes conformation, not connectivity.


`CH₃CH(CH₃)CH₂CH₃ = 2-methylbutane | CH₂=CHCH₂CH₃ = but-1-ene`


Common trap: An apparent bend in a chain is not automatically a branch. Find the longest connected path before naming.


Tutor explanation: Generate isomers systematically by changing the carbon skeleton, then check for duplicates using connectivity rather than the drawing’s orientation. C₅H₁₂ has pentane, 2-methylbutane, and 2,2-dimethylpropane. Reversing the page drawing of pentane does not create a fourth isomer. Restricted rotation at a C=C can also allow geometric isomers when each double-bond carbon has two different substituents; that is a different kind of isomerism from changing connectivity.


Check: Are butane and 2-methylpropane different formulas? Their molecular formula is the same, but their connectivity differs.

### 8.3 Functional groups: recognize, name & compare

A functional group is a recurring atom arrangement associated with characteristic chemistry. Recognize alkyl halides (C–X), alcohols (R–OH), ethers (R–O–R), aldehydes (R–CHO), ketones (R–CO–R), carboxylic acids (R–COOH), esters (R–COO–R), amines, and amides. Here R represents a carbon-containing group; an aldehyde may have H in place of that group in methanal.

Alcohol names use -ol, aldehydes -al, ketones -one, and carboxylic acids -oic acid. Esters are named with the alcohol-derived alkyl group followed by the acid-derived -oate. Functional groups influence polarity and hydrogen bonding. An alcohol’s covalent hydroxyl group is not a free hydroxide ion. A molecule may contain more than one functional group; simple naming rules need expansion for multifunctional compounds.


`CH₃CH₂OH: ethanol | CH₃COCH₃: propanone | CH₃COOH: ethanoic acid | CH₃COOCH₃: methyl ethanoate`


Common trap: An ether has C–O–C; a ketone has C–C(=O)–C. They are not the same functional group.


Tutor explanation: A carbonyl is C=O. Its location distinguishes aldehydes from ketones; attaching OH to the carbonyl carbon creates a carboxyl group. In an ester, the second oxygen connects the carbonyl group to a carbon group instead of to H. These local structures influence intermolecular attractions. Compare molecular size as well as functional group when predicting boiling point or water solubility; a long nonpolar chain can dominate the behaviour of a molecule with one polar group.


Check: Which class contains R–C(=O)–O–R′? An ester. R–O–R′ without the carbonyl is an ether.

### 8.4 Organic reactions, esterification & polymers

Complete combustion of many C/H/O organic compounds produces CO₂ and H₂O in sufficient oxygen. Substitution replaces an atom or group, as in halogenation of an alkane under suitable initiation. Addition converts a multiple bond into more single bonds to incoming atoms; hydrogenation adds H₂ across C=C. Elimination removes a small molecule and forms a multiple bond under suitable conditions.

Esterification combines a carboxylic acid and alcohol to form an ester and water, usually with acid catalysis and appropriate heating; the reverse process is hydrolysis. Addition polymerization links alkene monomers by opening their C=C bonds into a chain. Condensation polymers connect functional monomers while eliminating small molecules in suitable reactions. Identify the changed bond or group and preserve atom balance. Conditions determine which of several possible reactions actually occurs.


`CH₂=CH₂ + H₂ → CH₃CH₃ | CH₃COOH + CH₃OH ⇌ CH₃COOCH₃ + H₂O | nCH₂=CH₂ → [–CH₂–CH₂–]ₙ`


Common trap: The repeat unit of an addition polymer contains the monomer’s atoms, but the C=C becomes C–C connections along the chain.


Tutor explanation: Track the carbon skeleton before balancing H and O. Ethene addition retains its two-carbon framework; esterification joins an acid-derived acyl region to an alcohol-derived alkoxy region. A polymer’s properties depend on chain length, branching, and intermolecular packing as well as repeat-unit identity. More linear polyethylene chains can pack more efficiently than strongly branched ones, helping explain different densities. General organic reaction families describe patterns; catalysts, light, solvent, and temperature still matter.


Check: What type of reaction converts ethene to ethane using H₂ and a catalyst? Addition, specifically hydrogenation.

## Shared foundations


### Measurements, models & uncertainty

A measured result estimates a specified quantity under stated conditions. A calibration relates an instrument signal to a reference quantity. A measurement model y = f(x₁, x₂, …) connects the reported result with input measurements; its assumptions are part of the result. Standard uncertainty u(x) describes a standard-deviation scale, not a guaranteed maximum error. Random scatter and a shared calibration bias behave differently when measurements are averaged.

`For uncorrelated small uncertainties: u²(y) ≈ Σᵢ(∂f/∂xᵢ)²u²(xᵢ)`

The derivative is a sensitivity: it tells how strongly a small change in one input changes the answer. Correlated inputs add covariance terms. Significant-figure rules are useful reporting conventions, but they are not a full uncertainty budget.

Sources: Brown et al., Chemistry: The Central Science, 15th Global ed., §§1.6–1.7, measurement and dimensional analysis; printed p. 71 (PDF 73); NIST: combining standard uncertainties


### Amount, concentration & reaction extent

Amount n counts specified entities in moles. Avogadro’s constant links that count to particles. Molar mass M = m/n and molar concentration c = n/V are definitions with different denominators. Reaction extent ξ measures how far a balanced reaction has advanced. Assign signed stoichiometric numbers νᵢ: reactants negative, products positive.

`N = nNₐ; cᵢ = nᵢ/V; nᵢ = nᵢ,₀ + νᵢξ; dnᵢ = νᵢdξ`

Conservation connects all species through one ξ when only one independent net reaction occurs. Multiple simultaneous reactions need multiple extents. Amount is a physical quantity; a coefficient is a dimensionless number in the equation as written.

Sources: Brown et al., Chemistry: The Central Science, 15th Global ed., §3.4, mole and molar mass; printed p. 147 (PDF 149); Brown et al., Chemistry: The Central Science, 15th Global ed., §§3.6–3.7, stoichiometry and limiting reactants; printed p. 158 (PDF 160)


### Internal energy, work & enthalpy

Internal energy U is a state property. Heat q and work w describe energy crossing the system boundary; with the chemical sign convention, energy entering is positive. Enthalpy H = U + pV is another state property. For constant pressure with only pressure–volume work and appropriate mechanical conditions, its change equals the heat received.

`ΔU = q + w; w = −∫p_ext dV; H = U + pV; q_p = ΔH under the stated conditions`

A sample stores internal energy rather than “heat.” Exothermic describes heat release under the specified conditions; it does not determine reaction speed or establish whether every possible starting mixture reacts forward.

Sources: Brown et al., Chemistry: The Central Science, 15th Global ed., §5.2, First Law; printed p. 223 (PDF 225); Brown et al., Chemistry: The Central Science, 15th Global ed., §5.3, enthalpy; printed p. 230 (PDF 232)


### Entropy, chemical potential & equilibrium

Entropy is a state function that connects reversible heat transfer with temperature and, statistically, with the number of accessible microscopic arrangements. At fixed temperature and pressure, a spontaneous change with no imposed non-expansion work lowers the system’s Gibbs energy G = H − TS. Chemical potential μᵢ is the partial molar Gibbs energy: the change in G per small addition of species i while T, p, and other amounts are fixed.

`S = k_B ln W; μᵢ = (∂G/∂nᵢ)T,p,nⱼ; ΔᵣG = Σνᵢμᵢ; at equilibrium ΔᵣG = 0`

G itself need not be zero at equilibrium. The relevant reaction slope is zero, and a stable equilibrium is a minimum along the allowed changes. Equilibrium is a balance in thermodynamic tendency, while molecular forward and reverse processes can both continue.

Sources: Brown et al., Chemistry: The Central Science, 15th Global ed., §§19.2–19.3, entropy and microstates; printed p. 910 (PDF 912); Brown et al., Chemistry: The Central Science, 15th Global ed., §§19.5–19.7, Gibbs energy and equilibrium; printed p. 927 (PDF 929); Atkins & de Paula, Physical Chemistry, 8th ed., §5.1(b–c), chemical potential; printed p. 138 (PDF 170)


### Activity, logarithms & equilibrium constants

Activity aᵢ describes composition relative to a chosen standard state. On a concentration scale aᵢ = γᵢcᵢ/c°, with standard concentration c° and activity coefficient γᵢ. It is dimensionless. For an ideal gas, aᵢ = pᵢ/p°. Pure solid phases are assigned activity one in the usual standard-state treatment. A concentration is not always an adequate substitute for activity.

`μᵢ = μᵢ° + RT ln aᵢ; Q = ∏aᵢ^νᵢ; ΔᵣG = ΔᵣG° + RT ln Q; ΔᵣG° = −RT ln K`

These equations connect a mixture’s actual composition with its direction of net change. Q = K is the equilibrium condition. Concentration-based school expressions leave standard-state factors implicit and approximate nonideal interactions. The logarithm’s argument must be dimensionless.

Sources: Brown et al., Chemistry: The Central Science, 15th Global ed., §15.2, constants and dimensionless activity convention; printed p. 718 (PDF 720); Brown et al., Chemistry: The Central Science, 15th Global ed., §19.7, free energy and equilibrium; printed p. 938 (PDF 940); Atkins & de Paula, Physical Chemistry, 8th ed., §5.7, activities and standard states; printed p. 159 (PDF 191)


### Wavefunctions, probability & quantized energy

A quantum state is described by a wavefunction ψ. The probability of a position measurement in a region is the integral of |ψ|² over that region, with normalization over all space. Allowed stationary states solve an energy equation with boundary conditions. Atomic orbitals are one-electron spatial functions; a real many-electron state requires antisymmetry and electron interactions.

`Ĥψ = Eψ; P(region) = ∫region |ψ|² dτ; ∫all space |ψ|² dτ = 1; ΔxΔpₓ ≥ ℏ/2`

An orbital is neither a rigid container nor an electron trajectory. Positive and negative lobes mark wavefunction phase, not positive and negative charge. Quantization arises from allowed states and their boundary conditions rather than from a classical orbit being drawn at a convenient radius.

Sources: Brown et al., Chemistry: The Central Science, 15th Global ed., §§6.4–6.6, waves, orbitals and probability; printed p. 287 (PDF 289)


### Screening, electron configurations & bonding

In a many-electron atom, nuclear attraction competes with electron–electron repulsion. Effective nuclear charge is a useful screening model, not a literal reduction in proton count. Orbital energies depend on occupation; exchange and correlation help determine the lowest-energy electronic state. A molecule has orbitals extending over more than one nucleus, so isolated-atom orbitals are building blocks rather than the final electron distribution.

`Screening estimate: Z_eff = Z − S; MO bond order = ½(N_bonding − N_antibonding)`

Lewis structures conserve valence electrons; VSEPR predicts useful shapes; valence-bond and molecular-orbital models address overlap, delocalization, and electronic energies. Each model answers particular questions. None requires atoms to have a purpose such as “wanting” an octet.

Sources: Brown et al., Chemistry: The Central Science, 15th Global ed., §6.7, many-electron atoms; printed p. 300 (PDF 302); Brown et al., Chemistry: The Central Science, 15th Global ed., §7.2, effective nuclear charge; printed p. 326 (PDF 328); Brown et al., Chemistry: The Central Science, 15th Global ed., §§9.7–9.8, molecular orbitals; printed p. 445 (PDF 447)


### Energy landscapes, forces & molecular dipoles

For a coordinate r, the force along it is minus the slope of potential energy. A stable equilibrium separation has zero slope and positive curvature. Small displacements can be approximated by a harmonic well. Molecular dipoles depend on the complete nuclear and electronic charge distribution; bond dipoles provide a useful vector model.

`F = −dU/dr; near rₑ: U(r) ≈ U(rₑ) + ½k(r − rₑ)²; μ⃗_total ≈ Σμ⃗_bond`

Zero force does not mean zero motion: a vibrating pair can cross the energy minimum at maximum speed. Well depth concerns dissociation; curvature concerns vibration frequency. Bond polarity and molecular polarity are separate questions because vectors can cancel.

Sources: Brown et al., Chemistry: The Central Science, 15th Global ed., §9.4, bond energy versus separation; printed p. 429 (PDF 431); Brown et al., Chemistry: The Central Science, 15th Global ed., §9.3, geometry and polarity; printed p. 426 (PDF 428); Oxtoby, Gillis & Butler, Principles of Modern Chemistry, 8th ed., §3.7, forces from potential energy (PDF 124)


### Analytical concentration, species & charge balance

Analytical concentration records how much of a component was introduced per solution volume. Species concentrations record the chemical forms actually present after dissociation and reaction. Component balances conserve elements or chosen chemical components; bulk charge balance requires positive and negative charge concentrations to cancel. Equilibrium relations determine how conserved amounts are distributed among species.

`C_total = Σ(species contributions); Σzᵢcᵢ = 0; ionic strength I = ½Σzᵢ²cᵢ`

A 0.100 M CaCl₂ solution can have [Ca²⁺] ≈ 0.100 M and [Cl⁻] ≈ 0.200 M under complete dissociation. The sum of ion concentrations is not the analytical CaCl₂ concentration. Activities, complex formation, and weak ionization can require a more complete species model.

Sources: Brown et al., Chemistry: The Central Science, 15th Global ed., §4.5, electrolyte concentrations; printed p. 201 (PDF 203); Brown et al., Chemistry: The Central Science, 15th Global ed., §13.5, real electrolyte deviations; printed p. 640 (PDF 642); Brown et al., Chemistry: The Central Science, 15th Global ed., §16.7, ions reacting with water; printed p. 792 (PDF 794); Atkins & de Paula, Physical Chemistry, 8th ed., §5.9, ionic activities; printed p. 163 (PDF 195)


### Organic connectivity, orbital overlap & mechanisms

A molecular formula specifies composition; a structural formula adds connectivity; stereochemistry adds spatial arrangement. A σ bond has electron density along the internuclear axis in the usual localized description, while a π interaction uses sideways orbital overlap. Curved arrows in polar mechanisms track electron-pair redistribution from donor to acceptor; single-headed arrows track single electrons in radical mechanisms.

`For an ordinary neutral closed-shell formula: DBE = (2C + 2 + N − H − X)/2; O and S do not enter this count`

A ring or double bond contributes one degree of unsaturation; a triple bond contributes two. DBE constrains possible structures but does not identify one unique molecule. Mechanisms explain how bond patterns change and require suitable reagents and conditions.

Sources: Brown et al., Chemistry: The Central Science, 15th Global ed., §§24.1–24.6, organic structures and groups; printed p. 1149 (PDF 1151); Brown et al., Chemistry: The Central Science, 15th Global ed., §§26.1–26.3, π bonding and electron counting; printed p. 1210 (PDF 1212)


## 1. Skills & processes


### 1.1 A measurement is an instrument response plus a calibration

Say to students: “Choose the apparatus by the quantity you must know, then ask what its calibration and handling actually guarantee.”

- V_delivered; V_f − V_i: Delivered volume from two burette readings (mL or L)

- c = n/V: Amount concentration based on final solution volume (mol L⁻¹)

- δV: A small volume bias, distinct from random scatter (same unit as V)

1. Write the measurement equation. A pipetted analyte concentration inferred from titration is proportional to delivered titrant volume and inversely proportional to analyte aliquot volume. Apparatus errors enter that equation in different places.

2. Follow a specific bias. If a burette tip starts with an air bubble, some recorded delivery fills the tip rather than entering the flask. Treating the whole recorded volume as titrant that reacted overestimates analyte amount in a simple titration.

3. Connect technique to concentration. Residual rinse water inside a solution pipette can dilute its contents before the aliquot is taken. Extra pure water already in the receiving flask changes concentration but preserves analyte moles. These are physically different changes.

`c_analyte ∝ c_titrant V_delivered/V_aliquot; for a small volume error, δn ≈ c δV`

Worked check: With 0.1000 M titrant, an unrecognized 0.050 mL delivery bias corresponds to 5.0 × 10⁻⁶ mol. Against a 20.00 mL titre, the relative bias is about 0.25%.

Conditions: A glassware tolerance, an estimated reading uncertainty, and a delivery bias are different quantities. Temperature affects calibrated volumes; nominal values do not replace the actual calibration and prescribed drainage procedure.

Ask, then explain: If only the receiving flask is rinsed with pure water after a measured acid aliquot is added, must the equivalence titre change? No: the acid amount is retained unless material is lost or another reaction occurs.

Sources: Brown et al., Chemistry: The Central Science, 15th Global ed., §1.6, accuracy and precision; printed p. 71 (PDF 73); Brown et al., Chemistry: The Central Science, 15th Global ed., §4.6, titration analysis; printed p. 208 (PDF 210)


### 1.2 Inference, controlled variables & a quantitative model

Say to students: “A graph tests a relationship. Its slope, intercept, residuals, and uncertainties each tell us something different.”

- y = ax + b: A linear measurement model with slope a and intercept b (units of a are y-units/x-units)

- Residual y_i − ŷ_i: Observed value minus the model prediction (same unit as y)

- s/√N: Standard uncertainty of an average for independent repeat measurements (same unit as measurement)

1. State a falsifiable relationship. For a uniform material, measured mass versus sample volume can be modelled as m = ρV + b. The slope tests density; the intercept can reveal a container offset. A hypothesis should predict a pattern that competing explanations might fail to predict.

2. Separate repeatability from validity. Independent repeated trials reduce uncertainty in the mean roughly as 1/√N when the variation has a stable finite variance. They do not remove a shared calibration offset. A controlled experiment changes one relevant variable while checking plausible confounders.

3. Interpret a rate proxy carefully. In the sample magnesium–acid lab, reciprocal completion time is proportional to an average extent rate only when the same reaction extent is reached in every trial. A few times at different temperatures do not establish a universal maximum rate.

`m_measured = ρV + b; average progress rate = Δξ/Δt`

Worked check: If equal Mg pieces complete reaction in 40 s and 20 s, the second has twice the average progress rate under the same completion criterion. It need not have twice the instantaneous rate at every moment.

Conditions: Correlation is not sufficient for a causal claim. Linear regression needs a justified error model, and uncertainty in the x-variable can matter. The 1/√N improvement assumes independent trials rather than repeated readings sharing the same drift.

Ask, then explain: What does a constant vertical intercept change in a mass–volume plot? It changes absolute mass readings, but a fitted slope can still recover density if that offset is constant.

Sources: Brown et al., Chemistry: The Central Science, 15th Global ed., A Closer Look: scientific method; printed p. 63 (PDF 65); Brown et al., Chemistry: The Central Science, 15th Global ed., §14.2, average and instantaneous rates; printed p. 660 (PDF 662)


### 1.3 Derive uncertainty propagation instead of counting digits

Say to students: “Rounding states the precision you report. Propagation explains how input uncertainties affect the result.”

- u(x): Standard uncertainty of an input estimate x (same units as x)

- ∂f/∂x: Sensitivity of a result to an input, holding others fixed (result units/input units)

- cov(x,y): Shared variation between two input errors (units of x times units of y)

1. Linearize the measurement model. For small changes, δf ≈ Σ(∂f/∂xᵢ)δxᵢ. This is the first-order Taylor approximation: each input is weighted by its sensitivity.

2. Square and average. If the input errors are uncorrelated, cross terms vanish and their variances add. A product or quotient is especially simple after taking a differential of its logarithm.

3. Apply the quotient to density. For ρ = m/V, the derivatives are 1/V and −m/V². Dividing the resulting variance by ρ² gives the sum of squared relative standard uncertainties.

`u²(ρ) ≈ u²(m)/V² + m²u²(V)/V⁴
[u(ρ)/ρ]² ≈ [u(m)/m]² + [u(V)/V]²`

Worked check: For m = 10.00 g with u(m) = 0.01 g and V = 5.00 mL with u(V) = 0.02 mL, ρ = 2.000 g mL⁻¹ and u(ρ) ≈ 0.00825 g mL⁻¹. A suitable uncertainty report is approximately (2.000 ± 0.008) g mL⁻¹, with the ± identified as standard uncertainty.

Conditions: Add covariance terms for correlated inputs. Linear propagation can fail for large uncertainties, near-zero denominators, or strongly nonlinear models. A stated standard uncertainty is not a guaranteed error bound.

Ask, then explain: Can two measurements with many significant digits still be inaccurate? Yes; a shared calibration bias may preserve repeatability while shifting both results.

Sources: Brown et al., Chemistry: The Central Science, 15th Global ed., §1.6, reporting measurement precision; printed p. 71 (PDF 73); NIST: combining standard uncertainties


### 1.4 Density follows amount, packing & temperature

Say to students: “Density is a ratio. At molecular scale, it is the mass per mole divided by the space occupied per mole.”

- ρ; M; V_m: Density; molar mass; molar volume (g cm⁻³, g mol⁻¹, cm³ mol⁻¹)

- β: Volumetric thermal expansion coefficient: (1/V)(∂V/∂T)p (K⁻¹)

- Z: Gas compressibility factor PV_m/(RT) (dimensionless)

1. Introduce a mole-sized sample. Write m = nM and V = nV_m. Dividing cancels n, giving ρ = M/V_m. This explains why density depends on both particle mass and packing.

2. Connect temperature to density. At fixed mass, increasing volume lowers density. Differentiating ln ρ = ln m − ln V at fixed pressure gives d ln ρ/dT = −β. This is a local relationship, not a universal constant-density rule.

3. Recover a gas-density equation. For a gas V_m = ZRT/P, so ρ = PM/(ZRT). The ideal-gas expression follows only when Z is sufficiently close to one.

`ρ = M/V_m; (∂lnρ/∂T)p = −β; ρ_gas = PM/(ZRT)`

Worked check: For ideal CO₂ at 298.15 K and 100.0 kPa, M = 44.01 g mol⁻¹ and R = 8.314 kPa L mol⁻¹ K⁻¹ give ρ ≈ 1.775 g L⁻¹. The units are g/L, not g/mL.

Conditions: Use the density for the relevant phase, composition, temperature, and pressure. Liquid-mixture volumes need not be additive. Crystal packing and changes of solid structure can defeat a simple periodic density trend.

Ask, then explain: Why can a heavier element have lower density than a lighter one? Its molar volume can increase by an even larger factor.

Sources: Brown et al., Chemistry: The Central Science, 15th Global ed., §1.5, density; printed p. 67 (PDF 69); Brown et al., Chemistry: The Central Science, 15th Global ed., §10.4, gas densities; printed p. 487 (PDF 489)


## 2. The nature of matter


### 2.1 Macroscopic phases emerge from statistical particle behaviour

Say to students: “A phase is a collective state of many particles. A physical change can alter that collective arrangement while preserving chemical identity.”

- U; T: Internal energy; thermodynamic temperature (J; K)

- ⟨E_trans⟩: Mean translational kinetic energy of one ideal-gas particle (J per particle)

- Phase; component: Homogeneous physical region; chemically independent constituent (concepts with distinct roles)

1. Separate identity from arrangement. An H₂O molecule remains H₂O through melting and boiling. The distribution of particle positions and energies changes, so physical properties can change without a change in molecular formula.

2. State the kinetic model precisely. For a classical ideal gas at thermal equilibrium, mean translational kinetic energy is 3k_BT/2. Total internal energy can also include rotation, vibration, and interactions; the ideal-gas translational formula is not a general expression for every substance.

3. Use thermodynamics for phase choice. At fixed T and p, the stable phase has lower molar Gibbs energy. At coexistence the chemical potential of a component is equal in its coexisting phases. Equal phase potentials do not mean equal density.

`Ideal-gas translation: ⟨E_trans⟩ = 3k_BT/2; phase coexistence: μ_solid = μ_liquid`

Worked check: At one atmosphere near the normal melting point, ice and water can coexist while their densities differ. Supplying energy changes the phase proportions until the transition completes.

Conditions: Macroscopic phase models presume many particles and equilibrium or a specified metastable state. Nanoscopic clusters and rapidly driven samples may not follow simple bulk expectations.

Ask, then explain: Does equal temperature imply equal total energy in two samples? No: amount, composition, phase, and available energy modes can differ.

Sources: Brown et al., Chemistry: The Central Science, 15th Global ed., §11.1, molecular comparison of phases; printed p. 517 (PDF 519); Brown et al., Chemistry: The Central Science, 15th Global ed., §10.6, kinetic-molecular theory; printed p. 494 (PDF 496); Brown et al., Chemistry: The Central Science, 15th Global ed., §19.5, Gibbs criterion; printed p. 927 (PDF 929)


### 2.2 Build a heating curve from enthalpy changes

Say to students: “A temperature rise and a phase transition spend added energy in different ways. Add the correct energy terms along the path.”

- C_p; c_p: Heat capacity of a sample; specific heat capacity (J K⁻¹; J g⁻¹ K⁻¹)

- ΔH_fus; ΔH_vap: Molar enthalpy changes for fusion and vaporization (J mol⁻¹)

- ΔS_transition: Entropy change for a reversible transition at coexistence (J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹)

1. Integrate a heat capacity within a phase. At constant pressure with only pV work, dH = C_p dT. Integrate over the interval; if C_p is approximately constant, q_p ≈ C_pΔT = mc_pΔT.

2. Account for latent enthalpy separately. At the coexistence temperature, energy can change the fractions of two phases without changing T. A complete transition of n moles requires nΔH_transition.

3. Connect the plateau to equilibrium. For a reversible phase transition at T_tr, ΔG = ΔH − T_trΔS = 0, so ΔS_transition = ΔH_transition/T_tr. Coexistence, not absence of molecular motion, explains the plateau.

`q_total ≈ mc_sΔT_s + nΔH_fus + mc_lΔT_l + nΔH_vap + mc_gΔT_g
At coexistence: ΔS_transition = ΔH_transition/T_tr`

Worked check: For 1.00 mol of ice at 0 °C melting and then warming liquid water to 10 °C at about 1 atm: q ≈ 6.01 kJ + (18.02 g)(4.18 J g⁻¹ K⁻¹)(10 K) ≈ 6.76 kJ.

Conditions: Use the terms actually traversed, not all terms automatically. Assume fixed pressure, appropriate pure-substance transition data, negligible heat losses, and heat capacities valid over the chosen interval.

Ask, then explain: If the heating power doubles, must the melting temperature double? No. The ideal plateau becomes shorter in time while its coexistence temperature stays fixed at the same pressure.

Sources: Brown et al., Chemistry: The Central Science, 15th Global ed., §11.4, heating curves; printed p. 533 (PDF 535); Brown et al., Chemistry: The Central Science, 15th Global ed., §19.2, entropy and reversible heat; printed p. 910 (PDF 912)


### 2.3 Separation exploits unequal distribution between phases

Say to students: “A component moves differently because its interactions give it a different preference for each phase.”

- K_D = c_stationary/c_mobile: An idealized concentration partition coefficient (dimensionless for matching concentration units)

- R_f: Solute distance divided by solvent-front distance (dimensionless; condition-dependent)

- p_i = x_i p_i*: Raoult-law partial pressure in an ideal liquid solution (pressure)

1. Treat chromatography as repeated partitioning. A component repeatedly enters the moving and stationary regions. In a simple model, more material retained in the stationary phase means slower travel. The observed distance ratio is therefore linked to partitioning, not merely molecular size.

2. Derive a simplified retention relation. If k′ = K_D V_s/V_m is the ratio of stationary to mobile inventories and only the mobile inventory advances, the moving fraction is 1/(1+k′). Under restrictive planar assumptions this suggests R_f ≈ 1/(1+k′).

3. Explain distillation with vapour composition. For an ideal volatile mixture, vapour partial pressures are x_i p_i*. The vapour mole fraction y_i = p_i/Σp_j is enriched in the more volatile component. Repeated equilibration supplies fractional distillation’s benefit.

`k′ = K_DV_s/V_m; simplified R_f ≈ 1/(1+k′)
y_i = x_i p_i*/Σⱼ(x_jp_j*)`

Worked check: In a simple model, changing k′ from 1 to 3 lowers the predicted R_f from 0.50 to 0.25. This illustrates a stronger stationary-phase preference, not a universal calibration formula for paper chromatography.

Conditions: Adsorption, nonuniform flow, changing solvent composition, and overloading can invalidate the simple R_f relation. Real distillation can involve nonideal activities and azeotropes, so different boiling points alone do not guarantee complete separation.

Ask, then explain: Why does an R_f value need the solvent and stationary phase specified? Both change interactions and therefore the distribution between phases.

Sources: Brown et al., Chemistry: The Central Science, 15th Global ed., §1.3, separation of mixtures; printed p. 56 (PDF 58); Brown et al., Chemistry: The Central Science, 15th Global ed., §13.5, ideal volatile mixtures; printed p. 635 (PDF 637); Atkins & de Paula, Physical Chemistry, 8th ed., §5.3, ideal liquid solutions and Raoult’s law; printed p. 144 (PDF 176)


### 2.4 Formula writing is charge balance; a name encodes a chemical model

Say to students: “A name tells us whether we are counting atoms in molecules or neutral ratios of ions. These are different descriptions.”

- z_i; n_i: Signed ionic charge number; amount of ion i (dimensionless; mol)

- Oxidation state: Formal electron assignment using electronegativity conventions (not necessarily an actual atomic charge)

- Empirical formula: Smallest whole-number composition ratio (does not specify geometry)

1. Write electroneutrality explicitly. A bulk salt contains positive and negative ions in a ratio giving zero net charge. For x metal ions of charge +a and y anions of charge −b, ax = by. Smallest integers define the ionic formula unit.

2. Keep three charge ideas separate. An oxidation state assigns shared electrons to the more electronegative atom; formal charge splits shared pairs equally; actual partial charge reflects an electron-density partition and depends on its definition. Roman numerals name oxidation states, which equal ionic charges for a simple monatomic cation.

3. Locate the limits of naming shortcuts. “Metal plus nonmetal” is a first classification clue, not a theorem. NH₄Cl is ionic without a metal; covalent structures and coordination compounds need more information than a binary name. Molecular prefixes report composition but do not establish connectivity.

`Σᵢ zᵢnᵢ = 0; for Mᵃ⁺ and Xᵇ⁻, formula counts x:y = b:a reduced by their greatest common divisor`

Worked check: In Fe₂(SO₄)₃, sulfate contributes 3(−2) = −6. Two Fe atoms must contribute +6, so the simple ionic description uses Fe³⁺ and the name iron(III) sulfate.

Conditions: Charge balance alone does not prove a compound’s existence or stability. A molecular formula and a systematic name can still omit phase, solvation, or isomeric detail.

Ask, then explain: Can a neutral molecule contain atoms with nonzero formal charges? Yes. The formal charges must sum to zero, but individual assigned values may be nonzero.

Sources: Brown et al., Chemistry: The Central Science, 15th Global ed., §§2.7–2.8, ionic formulas and naming; printed p. 111 (PDF 113); Brown et al., Chemistry: The Central Science, 15th Global ed., A Closer Look: oxidation number, formal charge and actual charge; printed p. 392 (PDF 394)


### 2.5 Acid names, polyatomic ions & hydrate composition need species awareness

Say to students: “A formula tells the conserved composition. It does not promise that the same species remains intact in water.”

- HA; A⁻: Acid and its immediate conjugate base (differ by one H⁺)

- K_a: Equilibrium constant for acid proton transfer to water (dimensionless in activity form)

- Hydrate number x: Mole ratio H₂O to anhydrous salt in a specified hydrate (dimensionless)

1. Connect oxyanion naming to proton transfer. Sulfate and hydrogen sulfate are different species related by protonation. Their formulas and charges obey charge conservation, but the distribution in water depends on equilibrium, not just the compound’s original name.

2. Separate acid composition from acid strength. HCl(aq) names hydrochloric acid in water, where proton transfer is extensive under ordinary dilute conditions. A hydrogen-containing formula alone does not make every H equally acidic. K_a describes the transfer tendency in a specified medium.

3. Derive a hydrate ratio by material balance. If heating removes only water, mass lost divided by M(H₂O) gives moles of water. Divide by moles of remaining dry salt. The ratio identifies a stoichiometric hydrate if the solid reaches the intended composition.

`HA + H₂O ⇌ H₃O⁺ + A⁻; K_a = a(H₃O⁺)a(A⁻)/a(HA) with a(H₂O) ≈ 1
x = [(m_hydrate − m_dry)/M(H₂O)]/[m_dry/M(salt)]`

Worked check: If 2.50 g of a hydrate leaves 1.60 g of anhydrous CuSO₄, using rounded molar masses 18.02 and 159.6 gives x ≈ (0.90/18.02)/(1.60/159.6) ≈ 5.0, consistent with the pentahydrate within the stated precision.

Conditions: The gravimetric model fails if other volatiles leave, the salt decomposes, or the residue reabsorbs moisture. Acid constants depend on solvent, temperature, and standard-state convention.

Ask, then explain: Why does a hydrated salt need its full formula in a molar-mass calculation? Both its salt component and its crystal water contribute to the measured sample mass.

Sources: Brown et al., Chemistry: The Central Science, 15th Global ed., §2.8, acid naming; printed p. 121 (PDF 123); Brown et al., Chemistry: The Central Science, 15th Global ed., §16.1, proton transfer; printed p. 758 (PDF 760); Brown et al., Chemistry: The Central Science, 15th Global ed., §3.5, composition analysis; printed p. 152 (PDF 154)


## 3. The mole


### 3.1 An isotope average is an expectation value, not an individual atom

Say to students: “The periodic-table value summarizes a population. An individual atom has one isotope’s mass.”

- f_i; m_i: Fraction of isotope i; its atomic mass (dimensionless; u)

- m̄ = Σf_i m_i: Population-weighted mean mass (u per atom in this description)

- m/z: Mass-to-charge ratio measured in mass spectrometry (instrument convention, often expressed in Th)

1. Start with particle counts. For N_i atoms of isotope i, total mass is ΣN_i m_i. Divide by N_total and substitute f_i = N_i/N_total to derive the weighted mean. Fractional abundances sum to one.

2. Solve the inverse problem. For two isotopes, m̄ = fm₁ + (1−f)m₂. Rearranging gives f = (m₂−m̄)/(m₂−m₁). More than two unknown abundances require additional independent information; one average cannot determine an arbitrary mixture.

3. Connect the measurement to a spectrum. A mass spectrometer ionizes and separates species by m/z. Distinct isotopes can give distinct peaks. Peak areas require suitable corrections before treating them as isotope abundances; different charge states can create additional peaks.

`m̄ = (ΣN_i m_i)/N_total = Σf_i m_i
f = (m₂ − m̄)/(m₂ − m₁) for a complete two-isotope model`

Worked check: A hypothetical element has isotope masses 10.00 u and 11.00 u and mean 10.30 u. The lighter isotope fraction is (11.00−10.30)/(11.00−10.00) = 0.70, or 70%.

Conditions: Natural isotopic composition can vary by source, so a tabulated average is not a universal exact constant. Use actual isotope masses when supplied; proton-plus-neutron counts do not include nuclear mass defects.

Ask, then explain: Can two different three-isotope mixtures share the same average mass? Yes; the sum-to-one constraint and one mean leave one independent degree of freedom.

Sources: Brown et al., Chemistry: The Central Science, 15th Global ed., §2.4, atomic weights and the mass spectrometer; printed p. 101 (PDF 103)


### 3.2 The mole connects a discrete count with a macroscopic mass

Say to students: “A mole does not describe a kind of particle. It describes an amount of whichever entity you specify.”

- N_A: Exact conversion between amount and entity count (6.02214076 × 10²³ mol⁻¹)

- m_entity; M: Mass of one entity; molar mass of those entities (g per entity; g mol⁻¹)

- n_atoms = a n_molecules: Atom amount from a formula subscript a (mol of the named atoms)

1. Build mass from individual entities. For identical entities, m_sample = N m_entity. Since N = nN_A, the sample mass is nN_A m_entity; comparison with m_sample = nM gives M = N_A m_entity.

2. Track composition separately from amount. A mole of CO₂ molecules contains one mole of carbon atoms and two moles of oxygen atoms. Multiplying by a formula subscript changes the entity being counted; it does not change Avogadro’s constant.

3. Separate exact definitions from measured inputs. The modern SI fixes N_A exactly. An inferred particle count from a weighed sample still carries uncertainty from the sample mass, purity, and molar mass. Exact unit definitions do not make the experiment exact.

`m_sample = Nm_entity = nN_A m_entity = nM
N = (m_sample/M)N_A; N(O atoms in CO₂) = 2(m_sample/M_CO₂)N_A`

Worked check: A 0.0100 mol CO₂ sample contains 6.02 × 10²¹ CO₂ molecules to three significant figures and 1.20 × 10²² oxygen atoms. The factor two is exact for the specified formula.

Conditions: Mixtures require composition information rather than one assumed molecular mass. Isotopic composition changes molar mass slightly; ionic formula units and molecular entities must be named correctly.

Ask, then explain: Is 1 mol of O atoms the same sample as 1 mol of O₂ molecules? No. The latter contains twice as many O atoms and about twice the mass.

Sources: Brown et al., Chemistry: The Central Science, 15th Global ed., §3.4, mole and molar mass; printed p. 146 (PDF 148)


### 3.3 Derive the gas law from molecular momentum transfer

Say to students: “Gas pressure is momentum delivered to the container walls. Temperature sets the kinetic-energy scale in the ideal model.”

- N; m_p; ⟨v²⟩: Particle count; particle mass; mean squared molecular speed (entities; kg; m² s⁻²)

- k_B; R = N_Ak_B: Boltzmann constant; molar gas constant (J K⁻¹; J mol⁻¹ K⁻¹)

- Z = PV/(nRT): Compressibility factor measuring departure from ideal behaviour (dimensionless)

1. Relate wall impacts to pressure. In a classical dilute gas with isotropic velocities and elastic wall collisions, kinetic theory gives PV = Nm_p⟨v²⟩/3. Isotropy makes each spatial direction contribute one third of the mean squared speed.

2. Introduce thermal equilibrium. The mean translational kinetic energy per particle is m_p⟨v²⟩/2 = 3k_BT/2. Substituting gives PV = Nk_BT. Since N = nN_A and R = N_Ak_B, PV = nRT follows.

3. State how real gases depart. Intermolecular attractions can lower the pressure relative to the ideal prediction, while finite-size repulsions matter at high density. Z records the net deviation. The van der Waals equation adds a simple attraction correction and an excluded-volume correction.

`PV = ⅓Nm_p⟨v²⟩ = Nk_BT = nRT
(P + an²/V²)(V − nb) = nRT`

Worked check: At 273.15 K and 101.325 kPa, RT/P ≈ 22.41 L mol⁻¹. Changing only the standard pressure to 100 kPa gives about 22.71 L mol⁻¹. The 0.30 L mol⁻¹ difference is a convention change, not a change in Avogadro’s constant.

Conditions: Use absolute temperature and consistent units. The kinetic derivation assumes a dilute classical gas at equilibrium. The van der Waals model is approximate; it is not exact near all phase boundaries.

Ask, then explain: At the same temperature, do He and CO₂ have the same root-mean-square speed? No. Equal average translational energy gives a greater speed for lighter He particles.

Sources: Brown et al., Chemistry: The Central Science, 15th Global ed., §10.6, kinetic theory and the ideal gas equation; printed p. 494 (PDF 496); Brown et al., Chemistry: The Central Science, 15th Global ed., §10.8, real gases; printed p. 503 (PDF 505)


### 3.4 Composition analysis is an inverse problem with integer constraints

Say to students: “Mass percentages constrain atom ratios. Integer chemistry enters only after the masses are converted into amounts.”

- w_i: Mass fraction of element i (dimensionless; percentages are 100w_i)

- a_i; A_i: Integer atom count; atomic molar mass (dimensionless; g mol⁻¹)

- M = Σa_iA_i: Molar mass reconstructed from a proposed formula (g mol⁻¹)

1. Write the forward model. For a molecule with a_i atoms of element i, the mass fraction is w_i = a_iA_i/M. Dividing by A_i gives w_i/A_i = a_i/M, so these ratios are proportional to the atom counts.

2. Remove the common scale. Divide all element mole estimates by a common amount to obtain relative a_i. Values such as 1.50 require a common integer multiplier, rather than independent rounding of one element.

3. Validate the inferred formula. Recalculate predicted percentages from the proposed formula and compare with data and uncertainty. A poor match may indicate impurities, omitted elements, or an incorrect assumption about which elements are present. Formula finding should not force arbitrary noisy ratios into very large integers.

`w_i = a_iA_i/Σⱼ(a_jA_j); a_i:a_j = (w_i/A_i):(w_j/A_j)`

Worked check: For approximately 52.14% C, 13.13% H, and 34.73% O, a 100 g basis gives about 4.34:13.03:2.17 mol. Dividing by 2.17 gives 2:6:1, or C₂H₆O. This composition alone cannot distinguish ethanol from dimethyl ether.

Conditions: The sample must represent a single composition or a justified mixture model. Integer-ratio recognition must respect experimental uncertainty, and an empirical formula does not provide connectivity.

Ask, then explain: Why does multiplying every subscript by the same integer leave mass percent unchanged? Both each element’s mass contribution and total molar mass acquire the same factor.

Sources: Brown et al., Chemistry: The Central Science, 15th Global ed., §§3.3 and 3.5, elemental and molecular composition; printed p. 143 (PDF 145)


### 3.5 Combine elemental analysis with independent molecular information

Say to students: “One experiment can give a ratio; a second experiment can supply the missing scale.”

- k = M_molecular/M_empirical: Whole-number scale of a molecular formula relative to its empirical unit (dimensionless)

- n_C = n_CO₂; n_H = 2n_H₂O: Element balances in complete C/H combustion (mol)

- DBE: Degree of unsaturation, a formula consistency constraint (dimensionless integer for ordinary closed-shell structures)

1. Use combustion products as atom inventories. If all sample carbon enters CO₂ and all sample hydrogen enters H₂O, product amounts determine those element amounts. Oxygen in the products also comes from the oxidant, so sample oxygen requires an additional balance under a specified C/H/O-only assumption.

2. Recover the molecular scale. The empirical formula fixes relative atom counts. An independently measured molecular molar mass supplies k. Values far from a plausible integer indicate uncertainty, fragmentation in a mass spectrum, impurities, or a failed single-compound model.

3. Test chemical plausibility. A proposed neutral closed-shell C/H/O formula can be checked for a nonnegative integer DBE. This rejects some impossible formulas but cannot uniquely identify a structure. Hydrate ratios require a separate solid-state composition model, not an assumed gas-phase molecule.

`m_O,sample = m_sample − m_C − m_H for a C/H/O-only compound
molecular formula = (empirical formula) × k`

Worked check: An empirical unit C₂H₃O has mass about 43.04 g mol⁻¹. A measured molecular molar mass near 86.08 g mol⁻¹ gives C₄H₆O₂. Its DBE is (2×4+2−6)/2 = 2, so rings and/or multiple bonds must account for two unsaturation units.

Conditions: Combustion must be complete with products quantitatively collected. A mass-spectrum peak may be a fragment, adduct, or multiply charged ion; m/z is not automatically the neutral molecular mass.

Ask, then explain: Does a molecular formula fix which carbon carries a carbonyl group? No. It fixes elemental counts; connectivity and often spectroscopy are still needed.

Sources: Brown et al., Chemistry: The Central Science, 15th Global ed., §3.5, molecular formulas and combustion analysis; printed p. 154 (PDF 156); Brown et al., Chemistry: The Central Science, 15th Global ed., §32.5, molecular-formula deduction; printed p. 1487 (PDF 1489)


### 3.6 Concentration depends on a volume that itself depends on conditions

Say to students: “Molarity is convenient because it connects a measured aliquot to moles, but it is not the only concentration scale.”

- c = n_solute/V_solution: Molar concentration (mol L⁻¹)

- b = n_solute/m_solvent: Molality, with solvent mass in kg (mol kg⁻¹)

- x_i = n_i/Σn_j: Mole fraction including all counted components (dimensionless)

1. Derive the preparation equation. Given target c and final V, n = cV and m = nM combine to m = cVM. The final calibrated solution volume is the denominator; solvent volume alone is insufficient.

2. Explain temperature dependence. In a closed solution, n stays fixed while V can expand. Differentiating c = n/V gives dc/c = −dV/V; at fixed pressure this is dc/c ≈ −βdT for a small temperature interval.

3. Choose an alternative scale when useful. Molality uses solvent mass and avoids thermal volume expansion when composition is fixed. Mole fraction is natural for mixing and vapour-pressure models. Converting these to molarity requires density and a clear mass basis.

`c = n/V; m_solute = cVM; dc/c = −dV/V at fixed n
b = n_solute/m_solvent,kg`

Worked check: For 1.00 mol solute in 1.00 kg water, b = 1.00 mol kg⁻¹. The molarity is not automatically 1.00 M because neither adding solute nor mixing fixes the final solution volume at 1.00 L.

Conditions: Analytical concentration can differ from the concentration of an actual species after reaction or ionization. Accurate preparations use the calibration temperature, suitable solute purity, and appropriate hydrate molar mass.

Ask, then explain: If a sealed solution warms and expands without reaction, is solute amount lost? No. Its molarity decreases because the same amount occupies a larger volume.

Sources: Brown et al., Chemistry: The Central Science, 15th Global ed., §4.5, molarity and preparation; printed p. 201 (PDF 203); Brown et al., Chemistry: The Central Science, 15th Global ed., §13.4, concentration scales; printed p. 628 (PDF 630)


## 4. Chemical changes


### 4.1 A balanced equation is a conservation constraint, not a mechanism

Say to students: “Balancing asks whether the atom inventories match. It does not establish how the molecules react or how quickly.”

- ν_i: Signed stoichiometric number; negative for a reactant (dimensionless)

- A_ki: Number of atoms of element k in species i (dimensionless integer)

- ξ: Reaction extent for the equation as written (mol)

1. Express atom conservation. For every element k, ΣA_kiν_i = 0. Charge adds another conservation row for ionic reactions. Finding coefficients amounts to solving these simultaneous linear constraints.

2. Relate coefficients to amounts. Once an equation is fixed, a progress change dξ gives dn_i = ν_i dξ. Multiplying the whole equation changes the numerical definition of ξ without changing the physical conversion.

3. Separate overall bookkeeping from microscopic steps. An overall equation can sum many elementary steps. Its coefficients generally do not determine experimental rate-law powers. The equation also leaves out solvent, catalysts, and conditions unless they are supplied.

`For every conserved element k: ΣᵢA_kiν_i = 0; charge: Σᵢz_iν_i = 0
dn_i = ν_i dξ`

Worked check: For 2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O, ν = (−2,−1,+2). A change ξ = 0.10 mol consumes 0.20 mol H₂ and 0.10 mol O₂ and forms 0.20 mol water. Writing H₂ + ½O₂ → H₂O doubles the numerical ξ for the same physical conversion.

Conditions: One extent describes one independent net reaction. Multiple reactions need more independent variables, and a balanced equation can still describe a process that is unfavourable or kinetically inaccessible under the given conditions.

Ask, then explain: If 2A + B → products is an overall reaction, must its rate be k[A]²[B]? No. That requires suitable elementary-step kinetics or experimental evidence.

Sources: Brown et al., Chemistry: The Central Science, 15th Global ed., §3.1, conservation and equations; printed p. 134 (PDF 136); Brown et al., Chemistry: The Central Science, 15th Global ed., §14.6, elementary steps and mechanisms; printed p. 687 (PDF 689)


### 4.2 Reaction families organize patterns; energetics and pathways decide outcomes

Say to students: “Use a pattern to propose products, then check composition, conditions, and chemical driving force.”

- Δ_rG: Reaction Gibbs-energy change per mole of reaction as written (J mol⁻¹)

- Δ_rH: Reaction enthalpy change on the same reaction basis (J mol⁻¹)

- E_a: Arrhenius activation energy describing temperature dependence of a rate constant (J mol⁻¹)

1. Recognize the atom-rearrangement pattern. Combination, decomposition, and combustion organize many school equations, but they are not separate laws of nature. The same reaction can also be classified by energy transfer and by oxidation-state changes.

2. Ask whether the proposed products are favoured. At specified composition, Δ_rG = Δ_rH − TΔ_rS for a fixed-temperature comparison. Some decompositions are promoted at high temperature because gas formation contributes a favourable entropy term; enthalpy alone is insufficient.

3. Ask whether an accessible path exists. A favourable transformation can remain slow because it has a barrier. A catalyst changes kinetic access without changing the same reaction’s thermodynamic endpoint differences. Product identity can also change when oxygen supply or other conditions change.

`ΔᵣG = ΔᵣH − TΔᵣS; favourability and activation barrier answer different questions`

Worked check: For a hypothetical decomposition with Δ_rH° = +120 kJ mol⁻¹ and Δ_rS° = +0.150 kJ mol⁻¹ K⁻¹, the constant-parameter estimate gives Δ_rG° = 0 near 800 K. This does not guarantee a rapid decomposition at that temperature.

Conditions: The numerical crossover uses approximately temperature-independent enthalpy and entropy and standard-state quantities. Actual partial pressures change the reaction quotient, and side reactions may matter.

Ask, then explain: Why might a hot decomposition slow again if its gaseous product accumulates? The composition change can reduce the forward thermodynamic driving force.

Sources: Brown et al., Chemistry: The Central Science, 15th Global ed., §3.2, reaction families; printed p. 139 (PDF 141); Brown et al., Chemistry: The Central Science, 15th Global ed., §19.6, free energy and temperature; printed p. 934 (PDF 936); Brown et al., Chemistry: The Central Science, 15th Global ed., §14.5, activation; printed p. 681 (PDF 683)


### 4.3 Precipitation and displacement have different energetic tests

Say to students: “The activity series and solubility table summarize experiments. Their deeper explanations use redox energetics and ion activities.”

- Q_sp; K_sp: Current ion-activity product; equilibrium dissolution constant (dimensionless in thermodynamic form)

- E_cell: Reversible cell potential for a specified redox reaction (V)

- z_e; F: Electrons transferred per mole reaction; Faraday constant (dimensionless; C mol⁻¹)

1. Remove unchanged species. Splitting appropriate aqueous electrolytes and cancelling spectators reveals the net reacting species. This identifies whether the driving change is precipitation, proton transfer, gas formation, or electron transfer.

2. Interpret a solubility rule quantitatively. For MX(s) ⇌ M⁺ + X⁻, Q_sp = a(M⁺)a(X⁻). If Q_sp exceeds K_sp, dissolution has a positive reaction Gibbs energy and the reverse precipitation is favoured. Mixing first changes ion concentrations through dilution.

3. Interpret a metal activity ranking. For a feasible redox pair, Δ_rG = −z_eFE_cell links a positive reversible cell potential to favourable forward conversion. An activity series is a practical ranking under particular media and conditions. Passivation and water reactions can change observations.

`ΔᵣG_dissolution = RT ln(Q_sp/K_sp); ΔᵣG_redox = −z_eFE_cell`

Worked check: Mixing equal volumes of 0.010 M AgNO₃ and 0.010 M NaCl initially gives about 0.0050 M of each reacting ion. The concentration product 2.5 × 10⁻⁵ is far above an illustrative AgCl K_sp = 1.8 × 10⁻¹⁰, so precipitation is strongly favoured in the dilute model.

Conditions: Use activities for a thermodynamic constant; concentration products are approximations. Complexes, pH, coatings, solvent competition, and kinetic barriers can alter the simple prediction.

Ask, then explain: Can a formally written ion-exchange equation have no net reaction? Yes. If the same dissolved species remain, spectator cancellation can remove everything.

Sources: Brown et al., Chemistry: The Central Science, 15th Global ed., §§4.2 and 4.4, precipitation and activity series; printed p. 180 (PDF 182); Brown et al., Chemistry: The Central Science, 15th Global ed., §17.4, solubility products; printed p. 837 (PDF 839); Brown et al., Chemistry: The Central Science, 15th Global ed., §20.5, free energy and redox; printed p. 972 (PDF 974)


### 4.4 Electron bookkeeping connects oxidation states to electrical work

Say to students: “Oxidation states count a formal electron redistribution. When the process is arranged as a cell, that redistribution can deliver electrical work.”

- Oxidation state: Formal assignment that allocates heteronuclear bonding electrons by electronegativity (dimensionless)

- z_e: Moles of transferred electrons per mole reaction as written (dimensionless coefficient)

- F = N_Ae: Magnitude of charge carried by one mole of electrons (about 96485 C mol⁻¹)

1. Use a charge assignment consistently. Oxidation states sum to the species charge. Their changes count electron loss and gain in the formal model; actual partial atomic charges in a covalent molecule need not equal those integers.

2. Balance electron transfer. For Zn + Cu²⁺ → Zn²⁺ + Cu, the Zn increase 0→+2 matches the Cu decrease +2→0. Two electrons are transferred per equation advance. No free electrons remain in the final combined equation.

3. Connect to measurable work. Charge passed reversibly per mole reaction is z_eF, and potential is energy per charge. This gives Δ_rG = −z_eFE. Multiplying an equation multiplies Δ_rG and z_e together, leaving E unchanged.

`Q_charge = z_eFξ; ΔᵣG = −z_eFE
Oxidation-state increase × atom count = oxidation-state decrease magnitude × atom count`

Worked check: For a hypothetical two-electron reaction with reversible E = 1.10 V, Δ_rG ≈ −2(96485)(1.10) = −212 kJ mol⁻¹ of reaction. Doubling the equation gives twice the energy and electron count, still 1.10 V.

Conditions: Cell voltage depends on composition and temperature; tabulated standard reduction potentials require their standard-state convention. A favourable voltage does not establish a fast reaction at a passivated metal surface.

Ask, then explain: Why is an oxidizing agent reduced? It accepts the electrons lost by another species; its oxidation state decreases.

Sources: Brown et al., Chemistry: The Central Science, 15th Global ed., §4.4, oxidation numbers; printed p. 193 (PDF 195); Brown et al., Chemistry: The Central Science, 15th Global ed., A Closer Look: charge assignments; printed p. 392 (PDF 394); Brown et al., Chemistry: The Central Science, 15th Global ed., §20.5, electrical work; printed p. 972 (PDF 974)


### 4.5 Derive the calorimetry equation from the First Law

Say to students: “Enthalpy includes the energy associated with making space at constant pressure. That is why its change can equal the heat we measure.”

- U; H = U + pV: Internal energy; enthalpy of the system (J)

- q; w: Heat into the system; work done on the system (J)

- C_cal; c_soln: Calorimeter heat capacity; solution specific heat (J K⁻¹; J g⁻¹ K⁻¹)

1. Start with energy conservation. For a closed system, ΔU = q + w. If only expansion work occurs against a constant external pressure matching the relevant endpoint pressure, w = −pΔV.

2. Introduce constant-pressure enthalpy. At constant p, ΔH = ΔU + pΔV. Substitution cancels the work term and gives ΔH = q_p. At fixed volume with no other work, q_V = ΔU instead.

3. Balance the calorimeter’s energy. In an effectively isolated apparatus, heat received by reaction, solution, and cup sums to zero. Use q_reaction ≈ −(m_solnc_soln + C_cal)ΔT, then divide by the reaction extent or specified substance amount for a molar enthalpy.

`ΔH = ΔU + pΔV = q_p − pΔV + pΔV = q_p
ΔᵣH ≈ −(m_solnc_soln + C_cal)ΔT/ξ`

Worked check: A reaction warms 100.0 g of solution by 4.00 K. With c = 4.18 J g⁻¹ K⁻¹ and C_cal = 20.0 J K⁻¹, surroundings gain 1.752 kJ. If ξ = 0.0100 mol, the estimated Δ_rH is −175 kJ mol⁻¹, rather than the −167 kJ mol⁻¹ found by ignoring the cup.

Conditions: Check heat losses, evaporation, changing heat capacity, and reaction completion. ΔH is a state-function change; energy transferred as heat depends on the experimental constraint. Standard conditions do not automatically mean room temperature.

Ask, then explain: If a closed rigid vessel absorbs 500 J with no other work, what is ΔU? +500 J. ΔH additionally requires Δ(pV).

Sources: Brown et al., Chemistry: The Central Science, 15th Global ed., §§5.2–5.5, First Law, enthalpy and calorimetry; printed p. 223 (PDF 225)


### 4.6 One reaction extent generates every stoichiometric conversion

Say to students: “Mass, gas volume, and solution concentration are different measuring routes to the same conserved reaction progress.”

- ξ: Amount of reaction progress for the chosen equation (mol)

- ν_i: Signed stoichiometric number (dimensionless)

- r = (1/V)dξ/dt: Reaction progress rate per reacting volume (mol L⁻¹ s⁻¹)

1. Translate the measured quantity into amount. Use n = m/M, n = cV_solution, or n = PV_gas/(RT) under the appropriate model. Keep gas volume and reacting-solution volume distinct.

2. Eliminate the extent. If A is fully consumed with coefficient magnitude a, ξ = n_A,initial/a. Product B forms by n_B = bξ, yielding the familiar mole ratio n_B/n_A = b/a.

3. Extend the same definition to rates. Differentiate n_i = n_i,0 + ν_iξ. At fixed reacting volume, r = (1/ν_i)dc_i/dt. At changing volume, dc_i/dt also includes −c_i(dV/dt)/V, so a falling concentration need not imply chemical consumption.

`n_B = (ν_B/|ν_A|)n_A,consumed
dc_i/dt = (ν_i/V)dξ/dt − (c_i/V)dV/dt`

Worked check: For 2A → 3B at fixed V, A disappearing at 0.0200 M s⁻¹ corresponds to r = 0.0100 M s⁻¹ and B appearing at 0.0300 M s⁻¹. Those are different species signals for the same progress.

Conditions: The simple ratio assumes the specified reaction and amount actually converted. Multiple reactions, incomplete conversion, transfer between phases, or leaks require additional balances. Rate normalization depends on the equation as written.

Ask, then explain: Can adding pure water lower [A] without consuming A? Yes. It changes the volume term while ξ remains unchanged.

Sources: Brown et al., Chemistry: The Central Science, 15th Global ed., §3.6, stoichiometry; printed p. 158 (PDF 160); Brown et al., Chemistry: The Central Science, 15th Global ed., §14.2, rate and coefficients; printed p. 664 (PDF 666)


### 4.7 The limiting-reactant test is a constrained inventory problem

Say to students: “The reaction cannot consume more of any reactant than the amount available. The tightest inventory bound sets the maximum progress.”

- n_i,0; a_i: Initial reactant amount; positive reactant coefficient magnitude (mol; dimensionless)

- ξ_max: Largest progress compatible with nonnegative inventories (mol)

- Yield; conversion; selectivity: Recovered target/theoretical target; reacted feed/initial feed; fraction routed to a specified target (different dimensionless measures)

1. Write each remaining inventory. For reactant i, n_i = n_i,0 − a_iξ. The requirement n_i ≥ 0 imposes ξ ≤ n_i,0/a_i. Every reactant supplies one upper bound.

2. Take the smallest bound. The smallest n_i,0/a_i determines ξ_max. Product amount is bξ_max and each excess amount follows by subtraction. A tie means the available inventories are exactly stoichiometric within the model.

3. Locate purity and recovery correctly. A mass purity modifies the starting usable inventory before the bounds are compared. Actual conversion, competing reactions, and recovery losses can then lower isolated product. Percentage yield does not by itself distinguish those causes.

`ξ_max = minᵢ(n_i,0/a_i); n_product,theory = bξ_max
m_pure = f_pure m_sample; f_yield = m_actual/m_theory`

Worked check: For 2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O with 3.00 mol H₂ and 2.00 mol O₂: ξ_max = min(1.50,2.00) = 1.50 mol. Water formed is 3.00 mol and O₂ remaining is 0.50 mol. At 90.0% isolated yield, recovered water corresponds to 2.70 mol if it is pure.

Conditions: The bound is a stoichiometric maximum, not an equilibrium prediction. A low isolated yield may reflect incomplete conversion, side reactions, or handling loss; assuming a single cause needs evidence.

Ask, then explain: Could a reactant be in stoichiometric excess while some limiting reactant remains at equilibrium? Yes. “Limiting” identifies the complete-conversion inventory bound, not necessarily the actual equilibrium endpoint.

Sources: Brown et al., Chemistry: The Central Science, 15th Global ed., §3.7, limiting reactants and yield; printed p. 162 (PDF 164)


## 5. Investigating atoms


### 5.1 Atomic models are constrained by scattering, spectra & energy

Say to students: “A model earns its place by explaining evidence. A useful picture can still fail a more demanding experiment.”

- r; q_1; q_2: Separation and particle charges in a Coulomb model (m; C)

- U_Coulomb: Electrostatic potential energy relative to infinite separation (J)

- Observable: A quantity associated with a possible measurement (depends on the measurement)

1. Connect scattering to concentrated charge. A positive alpha particle passing near a small, strongly positive region can deflect substantially. The frequent near-straight trajectories and rare large deflections jointly constrain the nucleus to occupy a tiny fraction of the atomic volume.

2. Find the classical planetary model’s failure. A classical charge in a circular orbit accelerates and would radiate electromagnetic energy. It cannot explain stable atoms by simply treating electrons as small planets. Bohr imposed discrete stationary energies as an intermediate model.

3. Use the quantum state for a broader explanation. Quantum states predict distributions of measurement outcomes and discrete transitions. The nucleus can often be treated as relatively fixed while electronic states are calculated because nuclear masses are much larger; this is the basis of the Born–Oppenheimer separation in molecular modelling.

`U_Coulomb(r) = q₁q₂/(4πε₀r); like charges repel and unlike charges attract`

Worked check: An electron near a +e proton has U = −e²/(4πε₀r), whereas a +2e alpha particle near a +Ze nucleus has positive U = 2Ze²/(4πε₀r). The opposite signs explain attraction in one case and repulsive scattering in the other.

Conditions: Coulomb mechanics explains important scattering features but not a complete atom. Very close nuclear processes and quantum scattering need more than a classical point-charge picture. Born–Oppenheimer separation can fail when electronic and nuclear motion strongly couple.

Ask, then explain: Why did a successful nucleus model not finish the theory of the atom? It left electron stability, energy quantization, and detailed spectra unexplained.

Sources: Brown et al., Chemistry: The Central Science, 15th Global ed., §2.2, scattering and the nuclear atom; printed p. 92 (PDF 94); Brown et al., Chemistry: The Central Science, 15th Global ed., §§6.3–6.5, beyond Bohr; printed p. 281 (PDF 283); Oxtoby, Gillis & Butler, Principles of Modern Chemistry, 8th ed., §6.1, quantum picture and Born–Oppenheimer approximation (PDF 281)


### 5.2 Nuclide counts conserve charge, while nuclear mass includes binding energy

Say to students: “Mass number counts nucleons. It is not a precision mass measurement.”

- Z; A; q: Proton count; nucleon count; ion charge in units of e (dimensionless integers)

- B: Nuclear binding energy relative to separated nucleons (J or MeV)

- Δm: Mass difference associated with binding energy (kg or u with an appropriate conversion)

1. Use three independent counts. For a specified nuclide and charge, protons = Z, neutrons = A−Z, and electrons = Z−q. Ordinary ionization changes electron count, while a nuclear transformation can change Z or A.

2. Explain why measured isotope masses are not integers. A bound nucleus has less mass than its separated proton and neutron constituents. The difference is related to binding energy by B = Δmc². Atomic masses additionally include electron masses and their binding energies.

3. Distinguish isotope stability from a simple charge picture. The strong nuclear interaction provides short-range binding while protons repel electrically. Neutron-to-proton ratio and nuclear structure matter; describing neutrons as an attractive electric glue is inaccurate.

`neutrons = A − Z; electrons = Z − q; B = Δmc²`

Worked check: For ⁴⁰Ca²⁺, Z = 20 gives 20 protons, 20 neutrons, and 18 electrons. Removing those electrons does not convert calcium into argon even though the ion has an argon-like electron count.

Conditions: Use a specified isotope for neutron counts. Atomic mass tables average isotope populations; rounding a mean mass does not identify the nucleus in an arbitrary atom. Nuclear binding and chemical bonding involve very different energy scales.

Ask, then explain: Why do isoelectronic Ca²⁺ and Ar still have different properties? They have different nuclear charges and net charges despite the same electron count.

Sources: Brown et al., Chemistry: The Central Science, 15th Global ed., §§2.3–2.4, isotopes and atomic masses; printed p. 97 (PDF 99); Brown et al., Chemistry: The Central Science, 15th Global ed., §21.5, energy changes in nuclear reactions; printed p. 1031 (PDF 1033)


### 5.3 Derive a spectral wavelength from quantized hydrogen energies

Say to students: “A spectral line measures a gap between two allowed energies. Its photon is the energy difference carried away.”

- n_i; n_f: Initial and final hydrogenic principal quantum numbers (positive integers)

- Z: Nuclear charge number for a one-electron ion (dimensionless)

- R_H; h; c: Hydrogen Rydberg constant; Planck constant; vacuum light speed (m⁻¹; J s; m s⁻¹)

1. Use the one-electron energy model. To a useful approximation, E_n = −13.6 Z²/n² eV. The zero is a separated electron and nucleus, so bound-state energies are negative. The formula is exact only within the chosen nonrelativistic Coulomb approximation, with a reduced-mass correction to the constant for a specific nucleus.

2. Subtract endpoint energies. For emission n_i > n_f, ΔE_atom is negative and E_photon = E_initial − E_final is positive. Substituting the 1/n² energies gives a positive difference proportional to 1/n_f² − 1/n_i².

3. Convert energy into wavelength. Set E_photon = hc/λ and rearrange to the Rydberg form. This produces discrete wavelengths because n_i and n_f label discrete stationary states, not because the atom selects colours continuously.

`E_n ≈ −13.6 Z²/n² eV
1/λ = R_HZ²(1/n_f² − 1/n_i²); E_photon = hc/λ`

Worked check: For hydrogen emission 3→2, 1/λ ≈ (1.097 × 10⁷ m⁻¹)(1/4−1/9), giving λ ≈ 656 nm. The photon carries about 1.89 eV, while the atom loses that energy.

Conditions: The hydrogenic formula applies to one-electron atoms or ions, not neutral multielectron atoms using bare Z. Spectral intensities and allowed-transition rules require wavefunction matrix elements, not just energy gaps.

Ask, then explain: Does an absorption transition require an arbitrary small energy increase? Not for an isolated transition between bound stationary states; the absorbed photon must match an allowed gap within the line width.

Sources: Brown et al., Chemistry: The Central Science, 15th Global ed., §6.3, hydrogen energies and spectra; printed p. 281 (PDF 283); Brown et al., Chemistry: The Central Science, 15th Global ed., §6.4, wave behaviour; printed p. 287 (PDF 289)


### 5.4 Boundary conditions turn waves into discrete orbital states

Say to students: “The wavefunction gives probability amplitudes. Allowed shapes and energies come from solving a boundary-value problem.”

- ψ; |ψ|²: Wavefunction; probability density (density integrates to probability)

- n; ℓ; m_ℓ: Principal, orbital-angular-momentum and projection quantum numbers (dimensionless)

- ℏ = h/(2π): Reduced Planck constant (J s)

1. Use a simpler quantized system first. For a particle confined to a one-dimensional box of length L with impenetrable walls, ψ must vanish at both walls. Standing waves fit only when an integer number of half-wavelengths spans the box, so λ_n = 2L/n.

2. Derive its energy scale. Use p = h/λ and nonrelativistic kinetic energy E = p²/(2m). Substitution gives E_n = n²h²/(8mL²). The nonzero lowest energy follows from confinement; a constant nonzero wavefunction would violate the boundary conditions.

3. Return to atomic orbitals. A Coulomb potential in three dimensions has different eigenfunctions and energies. Its quantum numbers satisfy ℓ = 0…n−1 and m_ℓ = −ℓ…+ℓ. Hydrogenic angular and radial node counts are ℓ and n−ℓ−1. The probability in a shell includes a volume factor, so an orbital’s maximum density and most probable radius need not coincide.

`Box: ψ_n(x) = √(2/L) sin(nπx/L); E_n = n²h²/(8mL²)
Hydrogenic radial probability for 1s: P(r) = 4πr²|ψ_1s(r)|²`

Worked check: Halving the box length raises every energy level by a factor of four. In hydrogen, a 2p orbital has n = 2 and ℓ = 1, giving one angular node and no radial nodes.

Conditions: The box is an analogy, not an atomic potential. Orbitals describe one-electron functions; multielectron atoms require approximate treatments. Sketches of lobes do not determine n unless radial information or other labels are included.

Ask, then explain: Can an orbital have nonzero probability density at r = 0 but zero probability in a shell exactly at r = 0? Yes. The shell’s volume factor 4πr² goes to zero.

Sources: Brown et al., Chemistry: The Central Science, 15th Global ed., §§6.5–6.6, orbitals and radial probability; printed p. 291 (PDF 293); Oxtoby, Gillis & Butler, Principles of Modern Chemistry, 8th ed., §4.6, particle-in-a-box quantum model (PDF 208)


### 5.5 Configurations minimize total electronic energy, not a fixed orbital ladder

Say to students: “The filling diagram is a guide. The state belongs to the whole atom or ion, whose orbital energies depend on electron occupation.”

- Configuration: An orbital-occupancy description of an approximate many-electron state (not the complete wavefunction)

- Pauli exclusion: No two electrons in an atom share all four single-electron quantum labels (follows from fermionic antisymmetry)

- Exchange; correlation: Contributions associated with antisymmetry and coupled electron motion (important for total energy)

1. State the energy problem. The many-electron Hamiltonian includes attraction to the nucleus and repulsion between electrons. Approximate orbitals depend on the distribution of all occupied electrons, so their ordering is not a universal set of fixed shelves.

2. Interpret Hund and Pauli. Two electrons sharing one spatial orbital must have opposite spins. Within a degenerate set, the usual lowest-energy pattern occupies different orbitals with parallel spins before pairing. Exchange and electron repulsion supply the quantum basis; “electrons prefer empty seats” is only an analogy.

3. Build the ion from the actual neutral state. For transition cations, outer ns electrons are generally removed before (n−1)d. Cr and Cu exceptions reflect small competing total-energy differences, not a universal theorem that half-filled or filled subshells always override every other factor.

`Fe: [Ar]3d⁶4s² → Fe²⁺: [Ar]3d⁶ → Fe³⁺: [Ar]3d⁵`

Worked check: Co²⁺ is approximately [Ar]3d⁷ in a free-ion configuration. An isolated d⁷ subshell has three unpaired electrons under Hund filling; in a coordination complex, ligand-field splitting can change the occupation and magnetic behaviour.

Conditions: Core notation does not specify a complete atomic term or excited state. Coordination environments can change spin states, and orbital energies cannot be inferred from the written order of subshell symbols alone.

Ask, then explain: Why is an ion with 24 electrons not automatically configured like neutral Cr? Nuclear charge, electron interactions, and which outer electrons were removed differ.

Sources: Brown et al., Chemistry: The Central Science, 15th Global ed., §§6.7–6.9, many-electron atoms and configurations; printed p. 300 (PDF 302); Brown et al., Chemistry: The Central Science, 15th Global ed., §7.4, configurations of ions; printed p. 339 (PDF 341); Brown et al., Chemistry: The Central Science, 15th Global ed., §23.6, ligand fields; printed p. 1131 (PDF 1133)


## 6. Relationships & bonding


### 6.1 Periodicity comes from recurring valence states, not arbitrary table positions

Say to students: “The table’s pattern repeats because related outer electronic structures recur as nuclear charge increases.”

- n; ℓ: Principal shell and subshell angular-momentum labels (dimensionless)

- 2(2ℓ+1): Number of electron spin-orbitals within one subshell (2, 6, 10, 14 for s, p, d, f)

- Core versus valence: A useful division between relatively inert inner states and chemically accessible electrons (depends on the chemical question)

1. Count available states. A subshell has 2ℓ+1 orbital projection states and two spin possibilities per spatial orbital. The resulting capacities explain the broad widths of the periodic blocks.

2. Connect a main-group family to recurring chemistry. A recurring ns¹ or ns²np⁵ pattern gives related low-energy ways to lose, gain, or share electrons. The effective attraction and size still change down the group, so similar valence counts do not make reactivities identical.

3. Keep atomic and bulk properties separate. Electrical conductivity and density depend on extended solid electronic structure and packing. An atomic electron configuration helps interpret a trend but does not uniquely fix a material’s crystal structure, phase, or conductivity.

`For ℓ = 0,1,2,3: 2(2ℓ+1) = 2,6,10,14 electron states`

Worked check: Li ends 2s¹ and Na ends 3s¹. Their common outer occupancy helps explain +1 ions, while the larger, more shielded outer shell of Na helps explain a different ionization energy.

Conditions: Exceptions to simple filling occur, and transition-metal valence is more context dependent than main-group valence. A block label is a classification of the electronic pattern, not a statement that only one orbital participates in chemistry.

Ask, then explain: Why does the d block span ten columns? A d subshell has five spatial orbitals, each with two spin states.

Sources: Brown et al., Chemistry: The Central Science, 15th Global ed., §6.9, configurations and table structure; printed p. 309 (PDF 311); Brown et al., Chemistry: The Central Science, 15th Global ed., §7.7, group trends; printed p. 349 (PDF 351); Brown et al., Chemistry: The Central Science, 15th Global ed., §12.2, metallic electronic structure; printed p. 567 (PDF 569)


### 6.2 Ionization energy is a total-state energy difference

Say to students: “Across-period arrows summarize a competition between attraction, screening, subshell energy, and electron repulsion.”

- Z_eff = Z − S: Effective nuclear charge in a screening approximation (dimensionless)

- IE = E(X⁺) − E(X): Energy cost of electron removal with the free electron’s reference specified (energy per particle or per mole)

- Penetration: Orbital probability near the nucleus and inside shielding electron density (a spatial-distribution property)

1. Start with the attraction model. Across a period, increased nuclear charge is not fully screened by added same-shell electrons. Down a group, a new principal shell and additional screening generally increase size. Z_eff summarizes this competition but is not a single exact value for all positions in an atom.

2. Explain subshell exceptions. The electron removed from Al occupies a 3p state rather than Mg’s 3s. Lower penetration and the higher energy of that occupied p state can outweigh the simple increase in nuclear charge. Pairing in O’s 2p set also affects its total removal energy compared with N.

3. Compare whole states, not one orbital in isolation. Ionization lets the remaining electrons relax. A frozen-orbital picture can be useful qualitatively but is not an exact experimental IE calculation. Large successive-IE jumps indicate entry into a more tightly bound core.

`Rough hydrogenic estimate: E_n ∝ −Z_eff²/n²; experimental IE is a difference of relaxed total energies`

Worked check: Na⁺, Ne, and F⁻ have ten electrons. Their nuclear charges are 11, 10, and 9, so the usual isoelectronic size ordering is Na⁺ < Ne < F⁻, even though “same configuration” might suggest a superficial similarity.

Conditions: Atomic radius depends on its definition and bonding environment. Ionization-energy exceptions are not random; explain the relevant occupied subshell and electron interactions rather than applying one screening formula mechanically.

Ask, then explain: Why is the second ionization of an alkali metal much larger than the first? The second removal disrupts a closed inner-shell configuration instead of removing the outer ns electron.

Sources: Brown et al., Chemistry: The Central Science, 15th Global ed., §§7.2–7.4, screening, size and ionization; printed p. 326 (PDF 328); Oxtoby, Gillis & Butler, Principles of Modern Chemistry, 8th ed., §3.4, shell model and shielding (PDF 116)


### 6.3 An energy minimum predicts force, vibration & dissociation

Say to students: “Three questions require three features: slope gives force, curvature gives vibrational stiffness, and depth gives a dissociation-energy scale.”

- r_e; U(r): Equilibrium internuclear separation; potential-energy curve (m; J)

- k = U″(r_e): Local force constant at the minimum (N m⁻¹)

- μ_red = m₁m₂/(m₁+m₂): Reduced mass for relative two-body motion (kg)

1. Differentiate the energy landscape. Force along r is F = −dU/dr. At a stable equilibrium, U′(r_e)=0 and U″(r_e)>0. Attraction dominates on the gently sloping outer side; strong short-range repulsion dominates when atoms are pushed too close.

2. Expand about the minimum. Let x = r−r_e. The Taylor expansion begins U(r) ≈ U(r_e)+kx²/2 because the first derivative vanishes. It gives F ≈ −kx, the harmonic restoring force.

3. Introduce vibration. Relative nuclear motion has reduced mass μ_red, so its small-amplitude angular frequency is ω = √(k/μ_red). Quantum vibrational levels are approximately (v+½)ℏω above the well minimum. Zero-point motion makes the ground vibrational state lie above the potential minimum.

`F = −U′(r); U ≈ U(r_e)+½kx²; ω = √(k/μ_red)
Harmonic vibrational energies: E_v = (v+½)ℏω`

Worked check: If isotopic substitution doubles μ_red while the electronic force constant stays approximately unchanged, the predicted vibrational frequency decreases by 1/√2. A deeper well alone does not fix the frequency; the curvature matters.

Conditions: Harmonic behaviour applies near the minimum. Real bonds are anharmonic and dissociate at finite energy. The electronic potential curve usually invokes Born–Oppenheimer separation, and the energy to dissociate from the ground vibrational level differs from the full well depth.

Ask, then explain: Can a vibrating molecule have zero restoring force and nonzero nuclear speed at the same instant? Yes, when it passes through the equilibrium separation.

Sources: Oxtoby, Gillis & Butler, Principles of Modern Chemistry, 8th ed., §3.7, forces and bond potential energy (PDF 124); Brown et al., Chemistry: The Central Science, 15th Global ed., §9.4, overlap and bond separation; printed p. 429 (PDF 431)


### 6.4 Lewis drawings are electron accounts; molecular orbitals explain delocalization

Say to students: “A resonance contributor is a bookkeeping picture. The real electronic state is not a rapid sequence of those pictures.”

- Formal charge: Valence count − lone electrons − half shared electrons (integer bookkeeping value)

- σ; π orbitals: Common symmetry descriptions relative to an internuclear axis (spatial wavefunction character)

- Bond order: A model-dependent measure of bonding versus antibonding occupation (dimensionless)

1. Retain the electron-count constraint. A valid Lewis contributor preserves the total valence-electron count and atom connectivity. Moving nuclei creates an isomer or reaction, not merely another resonance contributor.

2. Explain delocalization beyond switching drawings. Overlapping orbitals can produce states spread across several atoms. In nitrate, a delocalized π system accompanies the σ framework and makes the equivalent N–O positions indistinguishable in the isolated symmetric ion. A naive localized single/double picture misses this equivalence.

3. Recognize where an octet picture fails. O₂ has two unpaired electrons in degenerate antibonding π orbitals and is paramagnetic, despite a simple paired-electron Lewis drawing. Hypervalent period-3 molecules also need more than “empty d orbitals make extra bonds”; modern calculations find little d-orbital participation in familiar cases such as SF₆.

`MO bond order = ½(N_bonding − N_antibonding)
For O₂: bond order = 2, with two unpaired π* electrons`

Worked check: For H₂, two electrons in a bonding σ orbital and none in σ* give bond order 1. Putting one additional electron into σ* gives H₂⁻ a simple MO bond-order estimate of ½. Stability still requires the full energy analysis.

Conditions: Formal charge and bond order depend on the chosen model and are not direct atom-by-atom charge measurements. Resonance weights need not be equal unless symmetry supports equality. Expanded-octet drawings remain useful composition and connectivity accounts.

Ask, then explain: Why does an O₂ Lewis structure alone fail to predict its magnetism? It hides the two unpaired electrons in the MO description.

Sources: Brown et al., Chemistry: The Central Science, 15th Global ed., §§8.5–8.7, charges, resonance and octets; printed p. 388 (PDF 390); Brown et al., Chemistry: The Central Science, 15th Global ed., §9.6, delocalized π bonding; printed p. 442 (PDF 444); Brown et al., Chemistry: The Central Science, 15th Global ed., §§9.7–9.8, MO theory and O₂; printed p. 445 (PDF 447)


### 6.5 Molecular polarity requires a vector sum and a three-dimensional structure

Say to students: “Equal polar bonds can cancel. The angle and symmetry determine whether the molecule keeps a net dipole.”

- μ⃗: Electric dipole vector of a neutral charge distribution (C m or debye (D))

- μ_b; θ: Equal bond-dipole magnitude; angle between the two bond-dipole vectors (C m; angle)

- sp, sp², sp³: Localized hybrid-orbital descriptions suited to some bonding geometries (basis choices, not separately observed objects)

1. State the complete dipole definition. The molecular dipole combines positive nuclear charges and the continuous negative electron distribution. A sum of localized bond dipoles is a useful approximation to that full distribution.

2. Derive the equal-bond result. For two equal bond vectors separated by θ, perpendicular components cancel and components along the angle bisector add. Each contributes μ_b cos(θ/2), giving μ_total ≈ 2μ_b cos(θ/2).

3. Connect geometry and orbital models carefully. Linear CO₂ cancels equal opposing bond contributions; bent water does not. VSEPR predicts useful arrangements. Hybrid orbitals offer a localized bonding representation, while molecular orbitals provide a different representation of the electronic state; observed geometry should not be justified by an unsupported circular “it is sp³ because it is tetrahedral” argument.

`For two equivalent bond dipoles: μ_total ≈ 2μ_b cos(θ/2)
At θ = 180°, cancellation gives μ_total = 0`

Worked check: For a bent equal-bond model with θ = 104.5°, the net magnitude is approximately 1.224μ_b. For the same two bond magnitudes arranged linearly, it is zero. Real water also reflects its complete electron distribution.

Conditions: The model assumes equal bond-dipole magnitudes and an appropriate vector representation. Different terminal groups can prevent cancellation even in a geometrically regular structure. VSEPR angles are predictions, not exact values for every molecule.

Ask, then explain: Would a linear O=C=S molecule have to be nonpolar? No. The two different bond contributions need not have equal magnitudes.

Sources: Brown et al., Chemistry: The Central Science, 15th Global ed., §9.3, molecular geometry and polarity; printed p. 426 (PDF 428); Brown et al., Chemistry: The Central Science, 15th Global ed., §9.5, hybrid orbital interpretation; printed p. 431 (PDF 433)


### 6.6 Intermolecular attractions have different physical origins and ranges

Say to students: “Every molecule has a fluctuating electron distribution. Permanent polarity adds interactions; it does not replace dispersion.”

- α: Polarizability: ease of distorting an electronic distribution (unit depends on convention)

- r: Separation between interacting particles (m)

- C₆: Coefficient of a simple long-range dispersion attraction (energy × length⁶)

1. Separate permanent from induced effects. Permanent dipoles interact with orientation-dependent energies. A nearby electric field can induce a dipole even in a nonpolar particle. Dispersion arises from correlated quantum fluctuations and is present for atoms and molecules generally.

2. Compare distance dependences within their models. At long range, an orientation-specific permanent dipole interaction scales as r⁻³. Its weak-interaction rotational thermal average can scale as −1/(Tr⁶). London dispersion commonly has −C₆/r⁶ form. The different formulas describe different constraints and averaging, not contradictory force laws.

3. Treat hydrogen bonding and bulk properties with context. Hydrogen bonds are directional interactions with electrostatic, polarization, and other electronic contributions. Boiling and solubility depend on the collective balance of many interactions, molecular shapes, and entropy. A force-name ranking alone cannot compare arbitrary molecules.

`U_dispersion(r) ≈ −C₆/r⁶ at sufficiently long range
A common illustrative pair model: U_LJ = 4ε[(σ/r)¹² − (σ/r)⁶]`

Worked check: In the attractive r⁻⁶ limit, doubling separation reduces the attraction magnitude to 1/64. The Lennard–Jones minimum lies at r = 2^(1/6)σ; σ itself is the zero-crossing separation, not the minimum location.

Conditions: Pairwise long-range formulas fail at short overlap distances and can miss many-body effects. The r⁻¹² repulsion is empirical, not a fundamental law. Strong ionic and hydrogen-bond networks need more than a simple one-site Lennard–Jones model.

Ask, then explain: Can a large nonpolar molecule boil at a higher temperature than a smaller polar molecule? Yes. Greater polarizability and collective contact can make dispersion substantial.

Sources: Brown et al., Chemistry: The Central Science, 15th Global ed., §11.2, dispersion, dipoles and hydrogen bonds; printed p. 520 (PDF 522); Oxtoby, Gillis & Butler, Principles of Modern Chemistry, 8th ed., §9.7, Lennard–Jones potential (PDF 467); Oxtoby, Gillis & Butler, Principles of Modern Chemistry, 8th ed., §10.2, origins of intermolecular forces (PDF 496)


## 7. Solution chemistry


### 7.1 Solubility is an equilibrium constraint on activities

Say to students: “Saturation is a balance of chemical potential. It need not mean that much solute dissolves.”

- s: Molar solubility: amount dissolved per solution volume (mol L⁻¹)

- K_sp: Equilibrium constant for a dissolution reaction (dimensionless in activity form)

- k_H: Henry coefficient in the specified c_gas = k_Hp_gas convention (concentration/pressure)

1. Write dissolution as a reversible reaction. For MX₂(s) ⇌ M²⁺ + 2X⁻, equilibrium requires Δ_rG = 0 and K_sp = a(M²⁺)a(X⁻)². The pure solid’s activity is taken as one; its quantity does not appear while that phase is present.

2. Translate stoichiometry into concentration. In initially ion-free water without competing reactions, [M²⁺] = s and [X⁻] = 2s. Under ideal dilute assumptions, K_sp ≈ (s/c°)(2s/c°)² = 4(s/c°)³. A common ion changes the inventories and destroys the simple 4s³ shortcut.

3. Separate gas and solid trends. A nonreacting dilute dissolved gas can follow Henry’s law at fixed T. Temperature changes equilibrium constants; the sign of dissolution enthalpy helps interpret the trend through d ln K/dT = Δ_rH°/(RT²), when the thermodynamic model applies.

`MX₂: K_sp ≈ 4(s/c°)³ → s ≈ c°(K_sp/4)^(1/3)
Henry convention here: c_gas = k_Hp_gas`

Worked check: For an illustrative MX₂ salt with K_sp = 4.0 × 10⁻¹², s ≈ 1.0 × 10⁻⁴ M in initially ion-free water. The anion concentration is 2.0 × 10⁻⁴ M, not s.

Conditions: Specify temperature, solid form, and free-ion activities. Complexes, acid–base reactions, and activity coefficients can change the calculated solubility. Henry constants have several inverse conventions; always inspect their equation and units.

Ask, then explain: If all solid has dissolved, does an unchanged mass of solid enforce saturation? No. The equality describes coexistence with the solid phase; an unsaturated solution can have no solid left.

Sources: Brown et al., Chemistry: The Central Science, 15th Global ed., §13.3, gas solubility and temperature; printed p. 621 (PDF 623); Brown et al., Chemistry: The Central Science, 15th Global ed., §17.4, K_sp and molar solubility; printed p. 837 (PDF 839)


### 7.2 Dissolution balances enthalpy with the entropy of mixing

Say to students: “Favourable solute–solvent attractions help, but mixing is decided by the full Gibbs-energy balance.”

- ΔH_soln: Enthalpy of forming the solution from specified separated components (J mol⁻¹ or J on a defined basis)

- ΔS_mix: Entropy change upon mixing (J K⁻¹)

- x_i: Mole fraction of component i (dimensionless)

1. Track interaction changes. Separating solute and solvent contacts requires energy in a simple interaction model, while forming new contacts releases it. The net dissolution enthalpy can have either sign.

2. Count the mixing advantage. For an ideal mixture of n moles, ΔS_mix = −nRΣx_i ln x_i. Since 0<x_i<1, each logarithm is negative and the ideal entropy of mixing is positive. For an ideal mixture ΔH_mix = 0, so ΔG_mix = nRTΣx_i ln x_i is negative.

3. Explain the useful heuristic’s limits. “Like dissolves like” often identifies favourable replacement interactions. Real mixtures can have excess enthalpy and entropy; unfavourable interactions can cause phase separation. Ionic dissolution also competes with lattice stability, and hydration can constrain local water arrangements.

`Ideal mixing: ΔG_mix = nRTΣx_i ln x_i; ΔS_mix = −nRΣx_i ln x_i
General fixed-T comparison: ΔG = ΔH − TΔS`

Worked check: For one mole total of two ideal components mixed equally at 298 K, ΔG_mix = RT ln(0.5) ≈ −1.72 kJ. A real system with a sufficiently positive excess mixing free energy need not remain a uniform single phase.

Conditions: The ideal formula assumes a well-defined ideal-mixture model. Ionic solutions require attention to species and electrostatic nonideality. More microscopic “disorder” does not automatically establish the sign of every real dissolution entropy.

Ask, then explain: Can an endothermic dissolution be favourable? Yes. A sufficiently favourable entropy term can outweigh its enthalpy cost at the specified temperature.

Sources: Brown et al., Chemistry: The Central Science, 15th Global ed., §13.1, energetics and mixing; printed p. 613 (PDF 615); Brown et al., Chemistry: The Central Science, 15th Global ed., §19.3, microstates and entropy; printed p. 915 (PDF 917); Atkins & de Paula, Physical Chemistry, 8th ed., §5.2(b), ideal mixing entropy and Gibbs energy; printed p. 143 (PDF 175)


### 7.3 Conduction, ionic strength & osmotic pressure probe different properties

Say to students: “Ion counts matter, but ion charge, mobility, and interactions determine what an electrical or osmotic experiment sees.”

- κ: Electrical conductivity of the solution (S m⁻¹)

- I = ½Σz_i²c_i: Ionic strength, expressed on the stated concentration scale (same concentration unit as c_i)

- i; Π: Effective van’t Hoff factor; osmotic pressure (dimensionless; pressure)

1. Distinguish dissociation from transport. Dissociation produces charge carriers. In a dilute transport description, conductivity is proportional to Σz_i²D_ic_i, with D_i a diffusion coefficient. Two solutions with the same number of ions can conduct differently because their mobilities and charges differ.

2. Compute the electrostatic concentration scale. Ionic strength weights each concentration by charge squared. For fully dissociated c mol L⁻¹ CaCl₂, I = ½(4c+2c)=3c. Charge balance instead uses signed z_i, giving 2c−2c=0; the two sums answer different questions.

3. Use colligative behaviour to test the independent-particle picture. For sufficiently dilute ideal solutions, Π ≈ icRT. Ideal complete dissociation gives i≈2 for NaCl and i≈3 for CaCl₂ relative to formal salt concentration. Real interactions and ion pairing can reduce the effective colligative factor.

`κ ≈ F²/(RT) Σᵢz_i²D_ic_i in a dilute independent-ion model with SI units
I = ½Σᵢz_i²c_i; Π ≈ icRT`

Worked check: For 0.0100 M CaCl₂, [Ca²⁺]≈0.0100 M and [Cl⁻]≈0.0200 M. Ionic strength is 0.0300 M, while the ideal total ion concentration is also 0.0300 M here for a different reason. They are not generally equal for other electrolyte formulas.

Conditions: The simple transport relation assumes weakly correlated dilute ionic motion; use c in mol m⁻³ for SI conductivity. The van’t Hoff factor is not always an integer and does not by itself identify a unique dissociation mechanism.

Ask, then explain: For 0.0100 M Na₂SO₄, is the ideal ionic strength 0.0100 M? No. It is ½[0.0200(1)²+0.0100(2)²] = 0.0300 M.

Sources: Brown et al., Chemistry: The Central Science, 15th Global ed., §4.1, electrolyte behaviour; printed p. 175 (PDF 177); Brown et al., Chemistry: The Central Science, 15th Global ed., §13.5, osmotic pressure and van’t Hoff factors; printed p. 639 (PDF 641); Atkins & de Paula, Physical Chemistry, 8th ed., §21.9(d), the Nernst–Einstein equation; printed p. 775 (PDF 807)


### 7.4 Dilution and mixing follow component balances before equilibrium

Say to students: “First conserve the chemical component. Then ask which species it becomes and which volume contains it.”

- C; c_i: Analytical component concentration; individual species concentration (mol L⁻¹)

- n_tot = ΣC_jV_j: Total introduced amount of a conserved component (mol)

- V̄_i: Partial molar volume: (∂V/∂n_i)T,p,others (volume mol⁻¹)

1. Derive the dilution relation. For a retained nonreacting solute, n_1=n_2. Substituting n=CV yields C₁V₁=C₂V₂. Species concentrations may still change differently if dilution shifts an ionization or association equilibrium.

2. Combine component inventories before dividing. For mixed feeds, n_total=ΣC_jV_j. The resulting analytical concentration is n_total/V_final. If a precipitate or gas removes part of the component from the measured liquid phase, account for that phase transfer before reporting dissolved concentration.

3. Treat mixture volume as a measured or modelled quantity. Real liquid mixtures do not always satisfy V_final=V₁+V₂. Partial molar volumes depend on composition and express how much the total volume changes when a component is added. Preparing to a volumetric mark avoids assuming volume additivity.

`Nonreacting dilution: C₁V₁ = C₂V₂
Mixed component: C_final = Σ(C_jV_j)/V_final; V = Σn_iV̄_i`

Worked check: Mix 10.0 mL of 0.200 M NaCl with 30.0 mL of 0.100 M NaCl. With additive volumes, n_total=0.00500 mol and C_final=0.125 M. The simple mean 0.150 M is wrong because the volumes are unequal.

Conditions: Conserve components rather than assuming every molecular species is conserved. Volume contraction, chemical reaction, volatilization, complexation, or precipitation can require additional terms. Use the actual final volume when available.

Ask, then explain: Can the formal concentration halve while a weak electrolyte’s ion concentration falls by less than half? Yes. Dilution can increase its fraction ionized, partly offsetting the analytical dilution.

Sources: Brown et al., Chemistry: The Central Science, 15th Global ed., §4.5, dilution; printed p. 204 (PDF 206); Brown et al., Chemistry: The Central Science, 15th Global ed., §13.4, concentration conversions; printed p. 628 (PDF 630); Atkins & de Paula, Physical Chemistry, 8th ed., §5.1, partial molar volume; printed p. 137 (PDF 169)


### 7.5 Titration equivalence is stoichiometry; the measured signal needs equilibrium

Say to students: “Equivalence counts matching amounts. The pH or indicator colour depends on the species remaining at that point.”

- V_eq: Titrant volume at the stoichiometric equivalence point (L or mL)

- K_a; K_b: Conjugate acid/base equilibrium constants (dimensionless in activity form)

- Endpoint: Observed criterion such as indicator colour (can differ from V_eq)

1. Derive the amount match. For aA+bB→products, the same extent gives n_A/a=n_B/b. At equivalence, C_AV_A/a=C_BV_eq/b. This calculation requires an effectively quantitative reaction on the experimental timescale.

2. Identify the species controlling pH. A strong-acid/strong-base equivalence mixture is approximately neutral under the usual dilute 25 °C model. A weak acid titrated with strong base leaves its conjugate base, which reacts with water; equivalence can be basic.

3. Estimate a weak-acid equivalence pH. For conjugate base A⁻ at analytical concentration C_s, K_b=K_w/K_a. Let x=[OH⁻]; if x≪C_s and water’s contribution is negligible, K_b≈x²/C_s, so x≈√(K_bC_s). An indicator endpoint should lie in the steep signal region near the intended equivalence.

`At equivalence: C_AV_A/a = C_BV_eq/b
Weak-acid equivalence estimate: [OH⁻] ≈ √[(K_w/K_a)C_s]`

Worked check: For a hypothetical weak acid with K_a=1.0×10⁻⁵ and equivalence salt concentration 0.0500 M, K_b=1.0×10⁻⁹ at 25 °C. The estimate gives [OH⁻]≈7.07×10⁻⁶ M, pOH≈5.15 and pH≈8.85. The changed fraction is only about 0.014%, consistent with neglecting x in C_s−x.

Conditions: The estimate assumes dilute ideal behaviour, a monoprotic system, no competing species, and an appropriate small-change check. Polyprotic titrations and overlapping reactions require a fuller balance model.

Ask, then explain: Why can c_AV_A=c_BV_B fail even if a colour change is sharp? The balanced reaction may not be 1:1, and the endpoint may not coincide exactly with the intended equivalence.

Sources: Brown et al., Chemistry: The Central Science, 15th Global ed., §4.6, titration stoichiometry; printed p. 208 (PDF 210); Brown et al., Chemistry: The Central Science, 15th Global ed., §17.3, weak-acid titration curves; printed p. 826 (PDF 828)


## 8. Organic chemistry


### 8.1 Carbon valence constrains formulas; σ and π bonding constrain geometry

Say to students: “A formula is an atom inventory. Valence adds structural constraints, and orbital overlap explains why some motions cost much more energy than others.”

- σ; π bonds: Head-on versus sideways overlap descriptions (local bonding model)

- DBE: Degree of unsaturation, counting rings and π-bond equivalents (dimensionless)

- C, H, N, X: Numbers of carbon, hydrogen, ordinary trivalent nitrogen, and monovalent halogens (atom counts in the formula)

1. Derive the saturated reference count. An acyclic carbon skeleton with C atoms has C−1 C–C single bonds. Those consume 2(C−1) carbon valence positions out of 4C, leaving 2C+2 positions for hydrogen.

2. Count missing hydrogen pairs. Each added ring or C=C uses two positions otherwise available to H; a C≡C uses four. With ordinary trivalent N and monovalent halogens, the extended count is DBE=(2C+2+N−H−X)/2. Divalent O does not change the hydrogen-count bound.

3. Connect multiple bonds to restricted rotation. A σ bond can maintain its principal overlap during rotation about its axis. A π interaction requires suitable alignment of neighbouring orbitals, so twisting a C=C disrupts overlap. This supports planar alkene geometry and possible E/Z stereoisomerism.

`Acyclic alkane: H_max = 4C − 2(C−1) = 2C+2
DBE = (2C+2+N−H−X)/2 under ordinary valence assumptions`

Worked check: C₄H₈ has DBE=1, compatible with one ring or one double bond. C₄H₆ has DBE=2, compatible with a triple bond, two double bonds, two rings, or one ring plus one double bond when chemically feasible.

Conditions: DBE does not identify a unique structure. The simple count assumes suitable neutral closed-shell valences; charged species, radicals, and unusual main-group valences can require a different analysis. Rotations about single bonds can still have torsional barriers.

Ask, then explain: Does DBE=1 prove that an alkene is present? No. A ring supplies the same unsaturation count.

### 8.2 Connectivity, conformation & stereochemistry are distinct levels of identity

Say to students: “Ask what has changed: the connections, a rotation, or a spatial arrangement that cannot be removed by ordinary rotation.”

- Constitutional isomers: Same molecular formula, different atom connectivity (distinct compounds)

- Conformers: Different accessible spatial arrangements of one connectivity, often linked by bond rotation (may interconvert rapidly)

- Enantiomers; E/Z isomers: Non-superimposable mirror-image pair; alkene geometric alternatives (stereochemical relationships)

1. Use a graph to recognize connectivity. Treat atoms as vertices and bonds as connections. Rotating or redrawing the graph does not create a new constitutional isomer. Naming rules seek an unambiguous description of that graph, subject to the relevant parent-chain and functional-group conventions.

2. Distinguish a rotation from a configuration change. Ethane staggered and eclipsed arrangements are conformations connected by rotation about C–C. Alkene E/Z alternatives usually require disrupting π overlap to interconvert. Each double-bond carbon must have two different substituents for E/Z alternatives to exist.

3. Add chirality. A tetrahedral carbon attached to four different substituents is a common stereogenic centre. Non-superimposable mirror images are enantiomers. Compounds can have different biological or physical interactions in a chiral environment even when formula and ordinary connectivity match.

`Structure hierarchy: composition → connectivity → stereochemical configuration → conformation`

Worked check: Butane and 2-methylpropane are constitutional isomers of C₄H₁₀. Rotation of a butane C–C bond gives conformers, not 2-methylpropane. But-2-ene has geometric alternatives, whereas propene does not because its terminal alkene carbon carries two H atoms.

Conditions: Not every carbon with four bonds is a stereocentre, and molecules with several stereocentres can have internal symmetry. Naming complex molecules requires full priority rules; simple “nearest branch” rules do not override a higher-priority functional group.

Ask, then explain: If two molecular drawings become identical after rotating the entire drawing or a freely rotating single bond, must they be different compounds? No. First compare connectivity and stereochemical configuration.

Sources: Brown et al., Chemistry: The Central Science, 15th Global ed., §24.3, conformations and constitutional isomers; printed p. 1155 (PDF 1157); Brown et al., Chemistry: The Central Science, 15th Global ed., §25.3, chirality; printed p. 1190 (PDF 1192); Brown et al., Chemistry: The Central Science, 15th Global ed., §26.2, E/Z isomers; printed p. 1217 (PDF 1219)


### 8.3 Functional groups organize electron density, resonance & acidity

Say to students: “A functional group predicts a pattern because it concentrates a recurring electronic structure in one part of the molecule.”

- Nucleophile; electrophile: Electron-pair donor; electron-pair acceptor in a reaction (roles depend on context)

- Resonance stabilization: Lower-energy delocalized electronic description relative to a restricted localized one (not oscillation between drawings)

- pK_a = −log₁₀K_a: Logarithmic measure of an acid equilibrium constant (dimensionless)

1. Read the carbonyl’s polarization. A C=O bond places electron density unevenly and creates a relatively electrophilic carbon. Nucleophilic addition at an aldehyde or ketone can be understood as forming a bond to that carbon while redistributing π density toward oxygen.

2. Compare related groups using resonance. A carboxylate ion distributes negative charge over two oxygen atoms in the symmetric limiting description, stabilizing the conjugate base. An amide lone pair interacts with the adjacent carbonyl system, giving the C–N bond partial double-bond character and changing nitrogen basicity compared with an ordinary amine.

3. Connect structure with acid strength and physical behaviour. A lower free-energy cost for proton transfer corresponds to a larger K_a. Hydrogen bonding, solvent stabilization, inductive effects, and molecular size all matter; naming a functional group does not supply an entire quantitative reactivity ranking.

`HA + B ⇌ A⁻ + BH⁺
K_transfer = K_a(HA)/K_a(BH⁺) = 10^[pK_a(BH⁺) − pK_a(HA)]`

Worked check: For two hypothetical acids with pK_a values 5 and 10 in the same solvent, transferring a proton from the pK_a 5 acid to the conjugate base of the pK_a 10 acid has K≈10⁵. The final amounts still depend on the initial inventories.

Conditions: Compare pK_a values only on a compatible solvent, temperature, and standard-state scale. Real functional-group behaviour also depends on substitution, conjugation, steric access, and reaction conditions.

Ask, then explain: Why is a hydroxyl group in an alcohol not simply a dissolved hydroxide ion? It is covalently bound in a neutral molecular structure; its ionization requires a separate chemical equilibrium.

### 8.4 Mechanisms explain the route; conversion and equilibrium control polymer size

Say to students: “A reaction-family name identifies the overall change. A mechanism tracks the electrons and intermediates that make that change possible.”

- Activation barrier; catalyst: A pathway’s kinetic obstacle; a species regenerated through an alternative path (separate from endpoint ΔG)

- p: Fraction of reactive functional groups consumed in an ideal step-growth model (dimensionless)

- X̄_n: Number-average count of starting monomer units per product molecule (dimensionless)

1. Distinguish pathways with the same overall pattern. Alkene addition can proceed by different mechanisms depending on reagents and conditions. Polar curved arrows track electron pairs from a donor bond or lone pair to an acceptor site; radical arrows track single electrons. Stoichiometric atom balance alone cannot select a mechanism.

2. Connect esterification to equilibrium. Acid catalysis changes the route of acid–alcohol esterification but not its equilibrium constant at a given temperature. Changing activities, such as by removing water under suitable conditions, can shift the attainable conversion.

3. Derive an ideal step-growth chain length. Start with N₀ difunctional monomer molecules in a balanced ideal system. Each intermolecular bond-forming step reduces the molecule count by one. At reacted functional-group fraction p, approximately pN₀ links have formed, leaving N=N₀(1−p) chain-bearing molecules, excluding small condensate byproducts. Thus X̄_n=N₀/N=1/(1−p). High chain length requires very high conversion.

`Ideal balanced bifunctional step growth: X̄_n = 1/(1−p)
At p=0.90: X̄_n=10; at p=0.99: X̄_n=100`

Worked check: Raising functional-group conversion from 95% to 99% increases the ideal number-average chain length from 20 to 100 units. This steep dependence helps explain why small stoichiometric imbalance, side reactions, or monofunctional impurities can strongly limit step-growth molar mass.

Conditions: The chain-length derivation assumes balanced bifunctional reactants, equal reactivity, negligible rings and branching, and no chain-stopping impurities. Chain-growth radical polymerization follows a different kinetic model and does not use this same conversion formula.

Ask, then explain: Does an esterification catalyst alone increase the equilibrium yield? No. It changes the rate of reaching equilibrium; changing composition or other thermodynamic conditions is a separate intervention.
